Summative Project Topics

Choose one topic for your group project. Your group will design an investigation, analyse data, and each write an individual policy report (1500–2000 words).

Choosing your topic

The topics suggested below are relevant to real-world decision-makers, and can be addressed using public datasets. Before selecting a topic, run round the PPDAC cycle a few times: can you find an interesting, well-defined research question within the topic; and are the available data suitable to testing a hypothesis relevant to your question?

1. Wastewater injection and earthquakes

Oklahoma went from a couple of felt earthquakes a year to hundreds, as the oil industry pumped its wastewater into deep wells. In 2016 the state regulator ordered injection cut. Did that work? The answer shapes how the UK will regulate the next things we pump underground: CO₂ storage, hydrogen storage and geothermal fluids. Who’s listening: the Oklahoma Corporation Commission; in the UK, the North Sea Transition Authority and the Environment Agency, which permit CO₂ storage.

Ways in:

  • Did the earthquake rate fall after the 2016 directives?
  • Does the earthquake count follow injected volume better with a lag?
  • Does injection depth (close to crystalline basement) matter as well as volume?

Data: USGS earthquake catalogue (ComCat), CSV for any box and period, e.g. Oklahoma, 2005–2025, magnitude 2.5+. Injection volumes per well: Oklahoma Corporation Commission, Oil & Gas Data (Excel, one file per year; formats change between years).

Watch out for: the oil price drove both injection and its fall, so “the directive worked” is confounded with “the market collapsed”. Aftershocks are not independent events. More seismometers after 2010 means more small earthquakes detected.

2. Quakes ‘n’ shakes: A “red light” for UK fracking?

Fracking at Preston New Road, Lancashire, was halted in 2019 after a magnitude 2.9 earthquake, under a “traffic light” rule that paused work at magnitude 0.5. Critics said quarrying and natural earthquakes routinely exceed that. Were they right? The moratorium was reaffirmed in 2024, and the same rules could govern geothermal and gas storage. Who’s listening: the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero (DESNZ), the North Sea Transition Authority.

Ways in:

  • How often does Britain have natural earthquakes above 0.5, 1.5 and 2.9? Fit the Gutenberg–Richter relation (log count against magnitude) and compare the Preston New Road sequence with it.
  • Did small earthquakes in the coalfields change after deep mining ended (Durham 1993, Kellingley 2015) and mine water rose?
  • Are induced earthquakes shallower than natural ones?

Data: BGS earthquake catalogue (search form with CSV output; 1970 onwards, plus a historical catalogue). One row per event: date, time, location, depth, magnitude.

Watch out for: the smallest magnitude the network reliably detects changed over time and differs by place (Blackpool was densely instrumented in 2018–19; rural Wales is not). Quarry blasts. Base rates.

3. Do earthquakes kill because of magnitude or poverty?

Aid budgets can pay for response after a disaster, or for prevention before it. Do wealthier countries’ higher investments in building regulations and infrastructure reduce earthquakes’ death tolls? Who’s listening: the Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office, the World Bank, national governments.

Ways in:

  • After allowing for magnitude and depth, do earthquakes kill fewer people in richer countries?
  • Has the death toll for a given magnitude fallen over the decades?
  • Do tsunami deaths follow a different pattern?

Data: NOAA NCEI Significant Earthquake Database (download from the search page; one row per event: date, location, depth, magnitude, deaths, damage). GDP per capita: World Bank (CSV).

Watch out for: an earthquake gets into this database if it does damage or kills people, so the sample is selected on the outcome. Population near the epicentre. Improvements to reporting over time.

4. Which volcanoes most deserve monitoring money?

Volcano observatories cannot watch everything. The 2010 Eyjafjallajökull eruption closed European airspace for days; some volcanoes with millions of people nearby have no monitoring at all. Records also seem to show eruptions becoming more frequent. Are they? Who’s listening: volcano observatories and their funders, aviation ash advisory centres, the UK Natural Hazards Partnership.

Ways in:

  • Has the number of recorded eruptions per decade risen? Is the rise in small eruptions (VEI 0–2), large ones, or both?
  • Which volcanoes combine large nearby populations with frequent eruptions?
  • Do some tectonic settings or rock types erupt more explosively?

Data: Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program: eruption search and volcano search, both with an Excel export (eruption dates, VEI; volcano type, setting, rock type, population within 5, 10, 30 and 100 km).

Watch out for: reporting bias in the eruption count, especially once satellites start capturing small, remote eruptions that would previously have been missed. Uncertainty in old eruption dates; how to handle an eruption that lasts decades.

5. Are abandoned metal mines still poisoning Pennine rivers?

Lead and zinc mines in Weardale, Allendale and the Nent valley closed a century ago, yet their drainage still supplies a large share of the cadmium, lead and zinc in England’s rivers, and makes many northern rivers fail their water-quality standards. Treatment schemes cost millions each, so they must be prioritized and shown to work. Who’s listening: the Water and Abandoned Metal Mines programme (Environment Agency, Mining Remediation Authority, Defra).

Ways in:

  • How much higher is dissolved zinc downstream of old mines than upstream, or than in similar unmined rivers?
  • Did zinc in the River Nent fall after its treatment scheme opened?
  • Does concentration fall at high flow (dilution) or rise (flushing)?

Data: Environment Agency Water Quality Archive (every sample since 2000; CSV download or API; one row per measurement: site, location, date, determinand, result).

Watch out for: values below the detection limit are reported as “< 5”, not zero. Sampling is not random (more samples during incidents). Wet years dilute, so a wet “after” period can fake a treatment success. Concentrations are skewed: work on a log scale.

6. Are floods on North East rivers getting bigger?

The “1 in 100 year” flood that decides where houses may be built, and how high defences go, is estimated from past peak flows on the assumption that the climate isn’t changing. If peaks are rising, we should be designing around larger floods. Who’s listening: the Environment Agency, planning authorities, the Flood Re insurance scheme.

Ways in:

  • Do annual peak flows on the Wear, Tees and Tyne show an upward trend?
  • Do CO2 concentrations explain flood intensity?
  • How much does one extreme winter (2015–16) change the estimated “1 in 100 year” flood?

Data: National River Flow Archive annual maximum flows, with catchment descriptors (area, rainfall, urban extent, permeability). Easiest via the R package UKFE: UKFE::GetDataNRFA(24001, Type = "AMAX") (24001 is the Wear at Sunderland Bridge).

Watch out for: gauges measure high flows least accurately. Urbanization.

7. Mine-water heat: is County Durham’s coalfield unusually warm?

The flooded coal mines beneath County Durham hold warm water that could heat homes: the Dawdon scheme near Seaham is being connected to a heat network planned to serve 750 homes (construction began in 2025). Is the North East’s mine water unusually warm? Who’s listening: the Mining Remediation Authority, Durham County Council, heat-network developers.

Ways in:

  • Does mine water warm faster with depth (a steeper geothermal gradient) in the North East than in South Wales or Nottinghamshire?
  • In blocks with both, is pumped water warmer than undisturbed water at the same depth?
  • How many homes could the North East’s warm blocks heat?

Data: Temperature Maps of the British Coalfields (Coal Authority and BGS), one row per Mine Water Block with temperatures every 100 m from 100 to 1000 m depth. No download button: read the query URL into R with jsonlite::fromJSON() and take $features$properties (swap Equilibrium for Pumped for pumped shafts). The Mining Remediation Authority Map Viewer shows each treatment scheme’s pumping rate and heat potential (Mine Water Heat → Waste Heat From Treatment). Method: Farr et al. (2021), The temperature of Britain’s coalfields.

Watch out for: temperatures at most depths are interpolated from a handful of readings, so a “temperature at 800 m” may be an extrapolation; keep only blocks marked “Measured”. Some readings date from working mines before 1925. Pumping draws deeper water up the shaft.

8. Where would deep geothermal heat pay off?

Deep geothermal wells cost millions, and pay off only where the Earth gives up its heat generously. The UK has drilled in Cornwall (granite), Newcastle (the Science Central borehole) and Weardale (Eastgate). Where should the next one go? Who’s listening: DESNZ, the Geothermal Energy Advisory Panel, city heat-network planners.

Ways in:

  • How does UK heat flow compare with countries that already use deep geothermal heat (Iceland, Germany, the Netherlands)?
  • Do older measurements, or poorer-quality ones, differ systematically?
  • Is heat flow higher over radiogenic granites than over sedimentary basins?

Data: Global Heat Flow Database, Release 2024 (International Heat Flow Commission / GFZ; about 91 000 measurements, one row each: location, heat flow in mW/m², depth, method, quality).

Watch out for: measurements cluster where people drilled for oil or minerals, not at random. Shallow boreholes are disturbed by past climate. Quality codes vary.

9. Who controls the minerals the energy transition needs?

Wind turbines, batteries and electricity grids need lithium, cobalt, rare earths and copper. The UK Critical Minerals Strategy judges a mineral “critical” partly by how few countries supply it. Stockpiling, recycling or mining at home (Cornish lithium, Weardale fluorspar) look more attractive when supply is concentrated. Who’s listening: the Department for Business and Trade, the UK Critical Minerals Intelligence Centre at BGS.

Ways in:

  • Has the top producer’s share (or a concentration index) risen for the minerals on the UK critical list?
  • If critical minerals are more concentrated than non-critical ones: does that reflect circular reasoning?
  • Do reserves sit in the same countries as production?

Data: USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026 data release (CSV; production, reserves and prices by commodity and country; earlier editions give a longer series). Longer history: BGS World Mineral Statistics (Excel export, one commodity at a time).

Watch out for: “critical” is defined partly by concentration. Some countries’ figures are estimates. By-product metals (cobalt from copper mines) depend on another market.

10. Does the rock beneath your home decide your radon risk?

Radon, a radioactive gas from uranium in rocks, is the second biggest cause of lung cancer in the UK after smoking. New homes need a radon barrier only in mapped “affected areas”, and free testing is targeted there. Do the maps, which follow geology, catch the homes at risk? Who’s listening: the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA), building regulations (Approved Document C), local councils.

Ways in:

  • Do indoor radon levels differ between areas on granite, limestone, mudstone and sandstone?
  • How many homes above the action level lie outside areas classed as high risk?
  • Is the North Pennines’ radon (Carboniferous limestone, Weardale granite) as high as Cornwall’s?

Data: UKHSA’s Radon in homes in England: 2016 data report (spreadsheet of results from over half a million homes, by local authority and postcode area: number tested, mean, maximum, number above the action level; Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own reports). The Indicative Atlas of Radon (BGS/UKHSA, Open Government Licence, via the OS Data Hub) gives the radon class of each 1 km square.

Watch out for: homes are tested because they are in suspected areas, so measurements are not a random sample. A district contains several rock types. Building type and ventilation matter. The Indicative Atlas gives each square its highest class.

11. Will the Chalk run dry?

Hosepipe bans and drought permits are triggered when groundwater at index boreholes falls below set levels. After the 2022 drought, did aquifers recover within a winter, and do some recover more slowly? Who’s listening: water companies (drought plans), the Environment Agency.

Ways in:

  • Did levels in the Chalk, the Sherwood Sandstone and the Magnesian Limestone recover at different rates after 2022 (or 2012, or 1976)?
  • How much does winter rainfall explain next summer’s groundwater level?
  • Are levels in the long-term records falling?

Data: Environment Agency Hydrology Data Explorer (groundwater levels at boreholes, CSV; filter stations by groundwater level). Rainfall: Met Office station data.

Watch out for: pumping near a borehole. Record lengths differ. Recovery depends on the following winter’s rain.

12. Is nitrate in our groundwater still rising?

Nitrate Vulnerable Zones restrict how farmers spread fertilizer over much of England, at real cost to farming. If groundwater nitrate hasn’t fallen, is that because the rules don’t work, or because water takes decades to soak down to the aquifer? Who’s listening: Defra (which designates the zones), water companies (who must treat or blend the water), the Environment Agency.

Ways in:

  • Are nitrate trends in Chalk or Magnesian Limestone boreholes still upward?
  • Are trends different inside and outside the zones?
  • Do boreholes with a thick unsaturated zone lag behind shallow ones?

Data: Environment Agency Water Quality Archive (groundwater sampling points, determinand “nitrate as N”); Nitrate Vulnerable Zones (NVZs) map. Context: Ascott et al. (2017), Global patterns of nitrate storage in the vadose zone.

Watch out for: today’s groundwater may record 1980s farming. The zones were drawn where nitrate was already high. Borehole depth.

13. Are glaciers shrinking fastest where people depend on them most?

In the Andes and Central Asia, glacier melt keeps rivers flowing through the dry season, supplying drinking water and irrigation to millions. Adaptation funding should go where loss is fastest relative to dependence. Who’s listening: development agencies, national water ministries.

Ways in:

  • Does the rate of glacier mass loss differ between regions?
  • Do small or low glaciers lose mass faster?
  • Has loss accelerated since 2000?

Data: World Glacier Monitoring Service, Fluctuations of Glaciers (mass balance and length change per glacier per year, CSV in a large zip).

Watch out for: monitored glaciers aren’t a random sample: they’re accessible, often small, and mostly in the Alps. Different measurement methods.

14. Does land use change how much carbon our soils hold?

The government now pays farmers to protect soil carbon. That makes sense only if land use measurably changes how much carbon topsoils hold. Who’s listening: Defra (Environmental Land Management), Natural England, the Climate Change Committee.

Ways in:

  • Do arable soils hold less carbon than grassland once soil pH is allowed for?
  • Did topsoil carbon change between the 1978, 1998 and 2007 surveys in the same squares?
  • How much of the land-use difference is really altitude and rainfall?

Data: Countryside Survey 2007 topsoil properties (UK Centre for Ecology & Hydrology; up to 591 1 km squares; one row per soil core: habitat, pH, carbon concentration, bulk density). You may need to accept a licence or register (free). 1998 data; 1978 data

Watch out for: land use goes with altitude, rainfall and rock type: peat is upland and wet, arable land lowland and dry. Carbon concentration is not carbon stock. Additional cores per square are not independent.

15. The gas price shock: did households really cut back?

Gas prices spiked in 2022 and the government capped them with the Energy Price Guarantee. Did households use less gas, and has use bounced back? Who’s listening: DESNZ, Ofgem (the price cap).

Ways in:

  • Did gas use per meter fall after 2022? Has it recovered?
  • Did the response differ between richer and poorer areas?

Data: Sub-national gas consumption statistics (DESNZ; by local authority, 2005 onward). Prices: quarterly energy prices.

Watch out for: a mild winter cuts gas use too. Insulation and boiler upgrades.

16. What’s behind the fall in UK electricity generation?

DUKES statistics show a sustained fall in UK electricity production over the past few years, despite economic growth, the adoption of electric vehicles, and a push to shift from gas to electricity for heating. What is behind this fall?

  • Are other electricity sources missing from this picture (overseas generation; rooftop solar that never reaches the grid)?
  • Has the decline in UK manufacturing reduced demand?
  • Have energy efficiency measures reduced domestic demand?

Data: DUKES Chapter 5 (generation, imports through interconnectors, consumption by sector). Energy Trends (quarterly). Manufacturing output: ONS Index of Production.

17. Has the fall in household electricity use ended?

Household electricity use fell for twenty years as lighting and appliances became more efficient. Heat pumps and electric cars will push it back up. Network operators must forecast when, and where first. Who’s listening: distribution network operators, the National Energy System Operator.

Ways in:

  • Has use per meter stopped falling? In which regions first?
  • Do areas with more heat pumps or EVs show it first?

Data: Sub-national electricity consumption statistics (DESNZ; by local authority, 2005 onward).

Watch out for: weather, prices, housing stock, population change, deprivation.

18. Do countries that build renewables have cleaner electricity?

Across countries, is more renewable capacity associated with less CO₂ per kWh? Who’s listening: international climate negotiators, aid programmes funding renewables.

Ways in:

  • Does the relationship survive allowing for GDP?
  • What happens to countries that rely on nuclear or hydro?

Data: IRENA renewable capacity statistics; Ember global electricity data (carbon intensity, generation mix).

Watch out for: nuclear (low carbon, not renewable); geography (Norway’s hydro); the ecological fallacy.

19. Offshore wind: are newer farms more productive?

Contracts for Difference auctions set the price offshore wind is paid, and bids assume a load factor. Are newer farms, with bigger turbines further offshore, delivering more? Who’s listening: DESNZ (auction design), the Crown Estate (seabed leasing).

Ways in:

  • Does load factor rise with commissioning year?
  • Is that the turbines, or the wind further offshore?

Data: Renewable Energy Planning Database (capacity, location, dates per project); generation from DUKES or Energy Trends.

Watch out for: distance from shore, water depth, curtailment. Linking the two datasets takes care.

20. Can old reactors bridge the gap to Hinkley Point C?

The UK’s ageing nuclear stations are closing faster than new ones open. Life extensions are keeping some running. Has output per GW held up as stations age? Who’s listening: DESNZ, EDF, the Office for Nuclear Regulation.

Ways in:

  • Has nuclear generation fallen in proportion to capacity?
  • Do load factors decline as stations age?

Data: DUKES Chapter 5 (generation by fuel; plant capacities).

Watch out for: planned outages, graphite cracking, demand.

21. Is Scottish wind worth the cost of getting it south?

Scotland is windier, but generators there pay more to use the grid, because most demand is in the south. Does higher output outweigh the charge? Who’s listening: Ofgem, the National Energy System Operator.

Ways in:

  • Do onshore load factors differ between UK nations or regions?
  • Is the difference big enough to matter, compared with grid charges?
  • Is electricity generated where it is used?

Data: Regional Renewable Statistics (DESNZ; capacity and generation by local authority).

Watch out for: turbine age, altitude, curtailment, weather year.

22. Is rooftop solar in the North worth the same as in the South?

Solar export payments are set nationally. If panels in Durham produce much less than panels in Devon, are northern households being short-changed, or the South over-rewarded? Who’s listening: Ofgem, DESNZ.

Ways in:

  • How much does solar output per MW fall with latitude?
  • Does latitude explain most of the variation, or does something else?

Data: Regional Renewable Statistics (DESNZ), with local authority latitudes; Regional consumption statistics record demand.

Watch out for: orientation, shading, cloud, roof vs ground mounting, unreliable reporting from small installations.

23. How fast is the UK installing heat pumps, and where can the ground do the work?

Heat pumps are central to decarbonizing heating. Ground-source pumps are more efficient but need suitable geology and space. Who’s listening: DESNZ (Boiler Upgrade Scheme), local councils.

Ways in:

  • Is installation accelerating? Does it differ by region?
  • Where do ground-source installations cluster, and why?

Data: MCS data dashboard (installations by technology and area); Boiler Upgrade Scheme statistics.

Watch out for: changing subsidies; housing type; gas grid connection; income.

24. Does burning waste reduce landfill?

Councils with energy-from-waste plants should send less to landfill. Do they, or does burning only replace recycling? Who’s listening: Defra, councils’ waste strategies.

Ways in:

  • Has landfill fallen faster in councils with EfW plants?
  • Do EfW councils recycle less?

Data: Local authority collected waste statistics (Defra; tonnes by destination per council per year).

Watch out for: waste crosses council boundaries, so define “has EfW” carefully.

25. Should planes burn extra fuel to avoid making contrails?

The Economist reports that diverting planes to avoid producing contrails – at the cost of a little extra fuel – could reduce the warming effect of contrails by more than half. But contrails are short-lived, whereas the additional CO₂ emissions endure. Over the long term, could such diversions do more harm than good? Who’s listening: the Department for Transport (Jet Zero), NATS (UK air traffic control); the European Commission, which has made airlines report their non-CO₂ effects since 2025 and must propose any measures to cut them by 2028.

Ways in:

  • How often is the air at cruise level over the UK cold and humid enough for persistent contrails (colder than about −40 °C, relative humidity over ice above 100 %)? Does it differ between seasons, locations, or between day (when a contrail reflects sunlight) and night (when it only traps heat)?
  • Flights through UK airspace fell by 87 % between January and April 2020. Did the day–night temperature range widen more than the weather explains? A much-cited study claimed it did after the US grounded flights in September 2001.
  • Is the trade-off worth it in the long haul? What does your answer depend on?

Data: Weather-balloon soundings: NOAA Integrated Global Radiosonde Archive, one zipped text file per station in data-por: Albemarle, Northumberland (UKM00003238, from 2002), Camborne (UKM00003808) and Lerwick (UKM00003005), both from the 1940s. Each sounding is a header line starting #, then one fixed-width line per pressure level (format): read the columns with substr(). Humidity comes as dewpoint depression; convert it to relative humidity over ice with the Magnus formulae. Flights: EUROCONTROL en-route traffic by FIR (one compressed CSV per year since 2013, which read.csv() opens as it is; daily flights through each country’s airspace, ENTITY_NAME “United Kingdom”, column FLT_ERT_1). Temperatures: Met Office station data (monthly maximum, minimum and rain; Durham from 1880). Forcing estimates: Lee et al. (2021), The contribution of global aviation to anthropogenic climate forcing. Compare your answer against Borella et al. (2024)

Watch out for: “half the climate effect” and “2 % more fuel” both need “compared to what?”. Spring 2020 was exceptionally dry and sunny, which widens the day–night range by itself.
