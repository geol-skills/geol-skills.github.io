Assessment Brief: Individual Policy Report

Overview

Weighting 30 % of module grade
Format Individual policy report, 1500–2000 words, in Markdown
Topic The topic your group chose
Submission report.md in your report repo (geol-skills-report-<username>), tagged final
Deadline

Monday 11 January 2027, 12:00 (UK time)

What you are writing

A policy report answering your group’s research question. Imagine your reader is a government adviser: busy, not a specialist, and needs to make a decision. Your report should tell them what you found, how confident they should be, and what it means.

This is individual work. Your group collaborates on data collection and analysis; you write up independently. Your report should reflect your own interpretation, your own figures, and your own conclusions — even if you used shared data and code.

Process

  1. Accept the assignment. Open the report assignment and accept it. This creates your own private repo, geol-skills-report-<username>. Clone it with GitHub Desktop.
  2. Write your report in report.md. A template file already exists in your repo. Don’t move or rename the file.
  3. Make each figure using a script in figures/, and embed the PNG it saves in report.md (see Format guidelines).
  4. Commit and push as you go. At the end of the Week 9 application session, your group repo’s history is copied into your report repo, so your group’s data and code sit alongside your report. Your own files are never overwritten. After that, the group repo is read-only.
  5. Submit by tagging your final commit final and pushing the tag, by the deadline. In GitHub Desktop: History → right-click the commit → Create Tag… → final, then Push. Pushing commits without the tag does not submit anything. Check that final appears under Tags on your repo’s GitHub page. Once you tag it as final, you can’t undo!

Structure

Your report should be 1500–2000 words, including figure captions but excluding code. Use the structure below:

1. Summary

Summary length: 200–300 words.

The main finding, the key evidence, and your recommendation. A reader who stops here should still know the answer.

2. Introduction

Introduction length: 200–300 words.

The question you investigated and why it matters. What motivated the investigation? What is the policy context?

3. Methods

Methods length: 250–400 words.

Your data sources, analytical approach, and the statistical tests you used. Enough detail that someone could reproduce your analysis for themselves.

4. Results

Results length: 500–750 words.

Your key figures (2–3), statistical test results, effect sizes, and confidence intervals. Interpret each result in plain language. Every figure should be referenced in the text and captioned with a “so what”.

5. Discussion and conclusions

Discussion and conclusions length: about 500 words.

What the results mean and what they don’t. Acknowledge limitations, assumptions, and potential confounders. State your policy recommendation and how confident you are in it. Communicate the uncertainty.

Format guidelines

  • Length: 1500–2000 words, including figure captions and excluding code.
  • File: write in Markdown, as report.md at the top of your report repo. The assessor will read Github’s rendering of the markdown document.
  • Figures: 2–3 well-chosen figures, each captioned and referenced in the text. Each figure must be made by its own script in the figures/ folder, named to match: figures/figure-costs.R creates figures/figure-costs.png. Scripts run from the top of your repo, so they read data from data/...; call set.seed() if a script uses randomness. Each script ends by saving its figure with ggsave(), e.g. ggsave("figures/figure-costs.png", width = 6, height = 4, dpi = 150) (see figures/figure-example.R in your repo). Embed the PNG with ![Alt text describing the figure](figures/figure-costs.png). Markers will re-run your scripts and check that each figure comes out the same.
  • Citations: Cite your data sources clearly. A simple inline citation is fine (e.g., “Source: DUKES 2026 Table 5.6B”). You do not need a formal reference list.

Rubric: What we are looking for

This report is your opportunity to demonstrate your ability to use scientific and statistical methods to design, implement and evaluate an effective experiment, and to convincingly communicate your findings. You will use these same approaches as you undertake your dissertation research project next year.

Your overall mark will reflect your knowledge and understanding of the course content, and the degree to which you have effectively applied the skills taught on the module.

A good report will satisfy these desiderata:

Criterion What this means
Clear research question The report addresses a specific, answerable question
Appropriate methods* Methods are described clearly; statistical tests match the data and question; assumptions are checked
Honest presentation* Figures are not misleading; numbers have context (“Is that a big number?”)
Effect sizes and uncertainty* The report says whether effects are consequential and trustworthy, addressing uncertainties within and outwith a model or test
Limitations acknowledged The report says what the analysis cannot tell us
Clear communication* A non-specialist can follow the argument from question to conclusion
Reproducibility Code is committed, runs, and reproduces the figures and results presented

How your mark is worked out

Step 1: Your starting mark

Your starting mark is calculated as your mean score across the primary criteria, marked with an * above.

Your work will be assessed against each of these categories on the University’s standard 100-point marking scale (Core Regulations, pp. 15–16)

Step 2: Adjustments for secondary criteria

The secondary criteria are each evaluated on a discrete scale:

Criterion Band descriptor Band range
Clear research question 3. Satisfied 65+
2. Partly satisfied 55–64
1. Loosely satisfied 40–54
0. Not satisfied 0–39
Limitations acknowledged 4. Concise and complete exploration of limitations 70+
3. Good summary of limitations 60–69
2. Limited acknowledgement of limitations 50–59
1. Limitations only implicitly acknowledged 40–49
0. No mention of limitations 0–39
Reproducibility: code 4. Code committed, runs, and reproduces all results 65+
3. Committed code relates to the results, but does not reproduce them faithfully 60–64
2. Committed code relates to the results, but does not run 50–59
1. Something irrelevant is committed 40–49
0. Nothing is committed 0–39

For each of these criteria, we identify the band your report reaches and compare it with your starting mark:

  • If your starting mark falls within the band range, no adjustment is made.
  • If your starting mark is below the band range, you have outperformed on this criterion, and gain 1 mark for each band above your starting mark.
  • If your band range is below your starting mark, you have not met the expected level on this criterion, and you lose 1 mark for each band below your starting mark.

Examples, for the research question criterion:

Starting mark Corresponding Research Question band Research Question band actually awarded Overall mark
39 0. Not satisfied (0–39) 2. Partly satisfied 39 + 2 = 41
70 3. Satisfied (65) 0. Not satisfied 70 − 3 = 67
64 2. Partly satisfied (55–64) 2. Partly satisfied 64

Step 3: Deductions

An AI use declaration is a required part of the assessment. If ai-use.md is missing or empty, 5 marks will be deducted.

Up to 5 marks may also be deducted for a clear failure to contribute to your group.

Group contributions

The report is your own individual work; but it draws on work you have done as a group. A group that’s working well will divide labour intelligently: perhaps one person might construct an analysis; another might check that its assumptions are met; and a third might give a detailed review on the resulting pull request. Each member’s contributions are reflected in their commit and contribution history on GitHub.

If you feel that a member of your group did not contribute fairly, contact the Chair of the Board of Examiners, James Baldini, or its Secretary, Janice Oakes, who will discuss the situation with everyone involved. If they find that contributions were clearly unequal, every member of the group will assess their own contribution and each other’s, using the group repo’s history as evidence. A student whose engagement fell well short of the rest of the group can then lose up to 5 marks from their overall mark.

Please raise any concern as soon as it arises, and ideally long before the deadline: the sooner it is raised, the more can be done to put it right.

Commit history matters

Your GitHub commit history shows how your work developed over time. We expect to see commits from multiple weeks — not a single commit the night before the deadline. The commit history:

  • Demonstrates your individual contribution
  • Provides evidence of genuine engagement (not just AI output)
  • Shows iterative development

A report with no commit history, or a single large commit, will raise questions about how it was produced.

AI use

Under the University’s policy on generative AI, AI use in this assessment is Selective: you may use it in the ways set out below, and no others.

You may use AI tools to help with coding, structure, and editing. But the assessment is designed so that generic AI output is insufficient.

Whether or not you use AI tools in your report, you must:

  1. Understand everything in your report. If asked, you should be able to explain any figure, test, or conclusion.
  2. Take full responsibility for the content of your report. AI makes confident errors and writes specious text. We expect that you will take intellectual ownership for the work done and its presentation. Code or text generated by AI tools may be a useful starting point, but the final code and text must be substantively your own.
  3. Not use AI to generate data or fabricate results.
  4. Commit an AI reflections document, ai-use.md, at the top of your report repo, beside report.md (see below).

Your AI reflections document: ai-use.md

Create a file called ai-use.md at the top of your report repo, and commit it with your report. Start it in the Week 9 application session, and add to it as you go. In it, describe:

  • which AI tools you used, and what for;
  • in general terms: What AI output you accepted, what you rejected, and what you modified, and why.

If you did not use AI at all, say so in ai-use.md, and describe the review you did instead in Week 9.

The purpose of this file is to contextualize your own contributions: there is no separate mark awarded for the AI use document itself. However, if ai-use.md is missing or empty, you lose 5 marks** from your overall mark, and your report is marked on the assumption that AI was used throughout. The file must be called exactly ai-use.md and sit at the top of the repo, not in a folder.

How we check that your code runs

To check reproducibility, an AI agent first launches a program that will re-run each of your figure scripts and compare what they produce with the figures and numbers in your report. Where its output does not match yours, a member of staff examines the discrepancy, re-running your code by hand if necessary, when determining your mark. No component of your mark reflects an AI judgement.

The agent is Claude Code, run on a Claude Team plan under Anthropic’s commercial terms. Your work:

  • is not used to train AI models;
  • is processed only to carry out the check, on our instructions;
  • is deleted from Anthropic’s servers after 30 days.

We will assume you consent to this. If you would rather your work were checked only by a person, e-mail Martin Smith, the module coordinator (firstname.lastname at durham.ac.uk), before the deadline; it will not affect your mark. Bear in mind that a busy person is a less patient re-runner of code than an AI agent! If you do opt out, we would be grateful to know why, so we can understand students’ concerns for future years.
