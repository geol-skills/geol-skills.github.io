How Biomass Electricity is Counted in Carbon Accounting

Under international carbon accounting rules (the UNFCCC framework), CO₂ released when biomass is burned for electricity is counted as zero.

This rests on a carbon cycle argument:

  1. Trees absorb CO₂ from the atmosphere as they grow.
  2. When the wood is burned, that CO₂ is released back.
  3. New trees grow in their place and reabsorb the CO₂.
  4. Over a full cycle, the net emission is zero.

On this basis, CO₂ from burning biomass is not counted against the country that burns it. The carbon is counted instead when the trees are felled, by the country that grows them.

In 2025, bioenergy accounted for about 14 % of UK electricity generation, around 40 TWh. Under the accounting rules, none of the CO₂ from burning this fuel appears in the UK’s reported emissions.

Key numbers

A tonne of pellets releases about 1700 kg CO₂ when burned and contains about 17 GJ of energy, so the fuel emits 100 kg CO₂ per GJ.

1 MWh = 3.6 GJ, so a power station converting heat to electricity at 36 % efficiency needs 3.6 / 0.36 = 10 GJ of fuel per MWh of electricity.

10 GJ per MWh × 100 kg CO₂ per GJ is 1000 kg CO₂ per MWh.

For comparison: coal ≈ 910 kg/MWh; gas ≈ 360 kg/MWh; wind ≈ 11 kg/MWh (lifecycle).

Wood contains more C–O bonds than coal (which is mostly C), so its carbon is already partly oxidized. Each carbon atom releases less energy when it burns, so more CO₂ goes up the chimney for every MWh generated.

Stage CO₂ (kg per MWh) Where it happens Where it’s counted
Combustion (what the chimney releases) 1000 UK Abroad
Harvesting + processing + drying 30–50 Abroad Abroad
Ocean shipping (US East Coast → UK) 15–25 At sea Internationally
Regrowth of forest −1000 Abroad Abroad

The country that grows the wood keeps land-use statistics that record the emission of carbon as forests are felled (captured by the “combustion” figure) and the capture of carbon as new trees grow (“regrowth”).

Fuel for international shipping is not allocated to a specific country; it is reported separately.

Balancing the books

The “count biomass emissions as zero” rule is necessary to avoid double-counting: the carbon loss is counted once when trees are felled, so mustn’t be counted again when pellets are burned.

Counting emissions as zero where the fuel is burned influences to whom emissions are attributed, but doesn’t affect the count of how much is emitted or captured.

Place Emitted there (kg/MWh) Counted there (kg/MWh)
UK 1000 0
Abroad −970 to −950 30–50
At sea 15–25 15–25
Balance 45–75 45–75

Regrowth of forest

The important rowvalue to focus on is the “regrowth of forest”. What assumptions are hidden beneath this figure?

Imagine burning a tree today, and planting new trees to capture the emissions. The new trees don’t capture all the carbon immediately. How long they take (the “payback period”) depends on what’s planted, and on how old the felled trees were. US Forest Service growth tables (Smith et al., 2006) for south-eastern loblolly pine give:

  • High-productivity plantation, felled at 25: about 25 years
  • Average stand, felled at 35: about 35 years
  • If the forest is not replanted: payback is never achieved

The UK’s net zero target is 2050. Biomass burned today is not carbon-neutral until its payback is complete.

Your task

Discuss as a desk. Using this summary:

  1. List the assumptions the “zero at combustion” accounting rule makes. (Aim for at least five.)

  2. For each assumption, ask: Is this testable? What evidence would challenge it?

  3. Which assumptions, if wrong, would most change the conclusion? Rank your top three.

You will use these answers in the application session, where you’ll test what happens when the assumptions change.
