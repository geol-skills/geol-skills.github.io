A model of gas: from a straight line to choosing a model

The live coding from Week 7: Models and their limits, to reproduce at home. How much UK electricity comes from gas, and what drives it? For gas a day at a time, see the extensions at the end of the exercise page.

A model of gas

elec has one row per year, 2000–2025; gas_twh is the electricity generated from gas.

1. How much of our electricity comes from gas?

Slide

  • lm(outcome ~ predictor, data = …) (new): fits a straight line, the linear model. It uses the same ~ formula as t.test().
  • coef(model) (new): takes a fitted model and gives its coefficients: here the intercept and the slope.
  • Already met: read.csv(), ggplot(), aes(), geom_point().
NoteHint 1
  1. A scatter plot: year across, gas_twh up. Going up, or down?
  2. Gas is the outcome, year the predictor. Store the model as model.
  3. Hand model to coef().
NoteHint 2 
ggplot(elec, aes(x = ______, y = ______)) +
  geom_point()

model <- lm(______ ~ ______, data = elec)
coef(model)
TipSolution 
ggplot(elec, aes(x = year, y = gas_twh)) +
  geom_point()

model <- lm(gas_twh ~ year, data = elec)
coef(model)

Slope −2.34: gas falls by 2.34 TWh a year. The intercept, 4836 TWh, is the line’s prediction for the year 0: where it crosses x = 0, far outside the data, and meaningless.

2. Could chance draw this line?

Slide

  • [["year"]] (new): takes one named element out of a list or vector, as a plain number. coef(model)[["year"]] is the slope alone.
  • Already met: coef(), lm(), sample(), set.seed(), replicate(), ggplot(), geom_histogram(), geom_vline(), mean(), abs() (Weeks 5–6).
NoteHint 1

The same five steps as Week 6’s boreholes: only the number changes, from δ to the slope.

  1. Take the "year" coefficient out of model.
  2. Shuffle gas_twh, fit lm() again, take its slope. Run it a few times.
  3. set.seed(7), then replicate() step 2 1000 times.
  4. A histogram, with slope and -slope marked.
  5. The fraction of null slopes at least as steep, either way.
NoteHint 2 
slope <- coef(model)[[<which one?>]]

coef(lm(sample(gas_twh) ~ year, data = elec))[["year"]]

set.seed(7)
null_slopes <- replicate(1000, <the line above>)

ggplot(data.frame(null_slopes), aes(x = null_slopes)) +
  geom_histogram(bins = 30) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = <both slopes>, colour = "red")

mean(<how many are this steep?>)
NoteHint 3 
slope <- coef(model)[["______"]]
slope

coef(lm(______(gas_twh) ~ year, data = elec))[["year"]]

set.seed(7)
null_slopes <- replicate(1000, {
  coef(lm(sample(gas_twh) ~ year, data = elec))[["year"]]
})

ggplot(data.frame(null_slopes), aes(x = null_slopes)) +
  geom_histogram(bins = 30) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = c(slope, -slope), colour = "red")

mean(abs(______) >= abs(______))
TipSolution 
slope <- coef(model)[["year"]]
slope

shuffled <- sample(elec$gas_twh)
coef(lm(shuffled ~ elec$year))[[2]]

set.seed(7)
null_slopes <- replicate(1000, {
  coef(lm(sample(gas_twh) ~ year, data = elec))[["year"]]
})

ggplot(data.frame(null_slopes), aes(x = null_slopes)) +
  geom_histogram(bins = 30) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = c(slope, -slope), colour = "red")

mean(abs(null_slopes) >= abs(slope))

Null slopes run from −2.0 to +2.6 TWh a year. Only 1 of 1000 is as steep as ours, so p ≈ 0.001. Shuffling treats the years as interchangeable; box 4 questions that.

3. What else does R know about the line?

Slide

  • summary(model) (new use): on a fitted model, gives a table of coefficients with their standard errors, t and p-values, then R² and the residual standard error.
NoteHint 1

One function, applied to model, gives all three.

TipSolution 
summary(model)
  • Slope −2.34 TWh a year, standard error 0.53: t = −4.38, p = 0.0002. The null world’s answer, from a formula.
  • R² = 0.44: the line accounts for 44 % of the year-to-year variation. 56 % is something else.
  • Residual standard error 20.4 TWh on 24 degrees of freedom (26 years, minus two coefficients): a typical year sits 20 TWh off the line. Is that a big number? Gas is about 130 TWh in a typical year.

4. Does a straight line fit?

Slide

  • plot(model, which = 1) (new use): on a fitted model, plot() draws diagnostic plots. which = 1 is residuals against fitted values; which = 2 is a QQ plot of the residuals.
NoteHint 1

plot(model) alone gives four plots. Pick one with which: the first, then the second.

TipSolution 
plot(model, which = 1)
plot(model, which = 2)

Residuals against fitted values should be a shapeless cloud. These aren’t: positive in 2007–10, sharply negative in 2012–15, positive again after. Neighbouring years are alike, so the residuals are not independent. (In 2012–15, cheap coal and expensive gas: generators switched to coal. Time causes nothing; the model has nothing else to go on.)

5. The line and its uncertainty, on the points

Slide

  • geom_smooth(method = "lm") (new): fits the line and draws it with its 95 % confidence band, in one layer.
  • predict(model, newdata, interval = …) (new, in the solution): the model’s prediction at new x values, with an interval.
  • Already met: ggplot(), aes(), geom_point().
NoteHint 1

geom_smooth() fits a line and draws its band. Tell it which kind of model with method.

NoteHint 2 
ggplot(elec, aes(x = year, y = gas_twh)) +
  geom_point() +
  geom_smooth(method = "______")
TipSolution 
ggplot(elec, aes(x = year, y = gas_twh)) +
  geom_point() +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm")

The band is where the line could be, not where next year’s point will be: most points sit outside it. Ask about 2026 both ways:

predict(model, data.frame(year = 2026), interval = "confidence")
predict(model, data.frame(year = 2026), interval = "prediction")

101 TWh either way, but 84–118 for the line and 56–147 for a single year.

6. A curve?

Slide

  • poly(year, 4) (new): year, year², year³ and year⁴ as four predictors, reworked so they don’t overlap. Inside a formula, it fits a degree-4 polynomial.
  • geom_smooth(formula = y ~ poly(x, 4)) (new use): draws a curve in place of a straight line.
  • Already met: lm(), coef(), summary(), ggplot(), geom_point(), geom_smooth().
NoteHint 1
  1. head() of poly(elec$year, 4).
  2. A new model, curve: gas_twh against poly(year, 4).
  3. geom_smooth() can draw any formula you give it, in terms of x and y.
NoteHint 2 
head(poly(elec$year, ______))

curve <- lm(gas_twh ~ ______, data = elec)
summary(curve)

ggplot(elec, aes(x = year, y = gas_twh)) +
  geom_point() +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm", formula = y ~ ______)
TipSolution 
head(poly(elec$year, 4))

curve <- lm(gas_twh ~ poly(year, 4), data = elec)
summary(curve)

ggplot(elec, aes(x = year, y = gas_twh)) +
  geom_point() +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm", formula = y ~ poly(x, 4))

R² 0.55, against the line’s 0.44. Why not add year², year³ and year⁴ yourself? Over 2000–2025 they are almost the same variable: try lm(gas_twh ~ year + I(year^2) + I(year^3) + I(year^4), data = elec), and the year³ term comes back NA, dropped without a warning. poly() avoids that, at a price: its coefficients mean nothing on their own. The first, −89.4, is not a slope in TWh per year.

7. Is the curve better?

Slide

  • summary(model)$r.squared (new): takes just the R² out of the summary.
  • predict(model, newdata) (new use): predictions for rows you supply, such as years the model never saw.
  • Already met: data.frame(), c().
NoteHint 1
  1. R² for model, then for curve.
  2. A data frame with one column, year, holding 2030 and 2040. Hand it to predict() with each model.
NoteHint 2 
summary(model)$______
summary(curve)$______

future <- data.frame(year = c(______, ______))
predict(model, future)
predict(curve, future)
TipSolution 
summary(model)$r.squared   # 0.444
summary(curve)$r.squared   # 0.552

future <- data.frame(year = c(2030, 2040))
predict(model, future)     # 92.1, 68.7 TWh
predict(curve, future)     # −67.9, −1356 TWh

R² always rises when you add terms, even useless ones. Extrapolated, the curve takes gas below zero in 2028; the line still has 69 TWh in 2040. Same data, very different futures. Which do you believe? What is the model not capturing?

Choosing a model

The line misses the 2012–15 trough, and the curve explains more. But R² always rises as terms are added. Which model is better, and what are both of them missing?

8. A fair comparison: AIC

Slide

  • AIC(model1, model2, …) (new): scores each model’s fit, with a penalty of 2 for every coefficient. Lower is better; only differences matter, and only between models of the same data.
  • Already met: lm(), poly().
NoteHint 1
  1. Hand AIC() both models.
  2. Fit gas_twh ~ poly(year, 2) and take its AIC. Then 3, 5, 8, 12.
NoteHint 2 
AIC(______, ______)
AIC(lm(gas_twh ~ poly(year, ______), data = elec))
TipSolution 
AIC(model, curve)
AIC(lm(gas_twh ~ poly(year, 2), data = elec))
Degree 1 2 3 4 5 6 8 10 12
AIC 234.5 235.3 236.7 234.9 234.2 230.3 225.8 209.7 201.4

Degrees 1 to 5 are a tie: AIC differences under about 2 mean nothing, and above about 10 they are decisive. The curve’s extra R² was not worth its three extra terms.

Push on, and AIC keeps falling: by degree 12, 13 coefficients for 26 years. With so few points AIC’s penalty is gentle, but the wiggles are also catching something real: the 2012–15 trough. The year can’t cause a trough. What can?

9. Did the extra terms help?

Slide

elec now also holds things that might drive gas: the gas price (gas_price, p/kWh), the Central England temperature (temp_c), net electricity imports (imports_twh), GDP (gdp, £ billion), and two TRUE/FALSE columns, covid (2020–21) and carbon_floor (from 2013, when the UK’s Carbon Price Floor began).

  • names(df) (new): the column names of a data frame.
  • merge(a, b) (in the setup): joins two data frames on the columns they share, here year.
  • Already met: lm(), + in a formula, summary(), AIC().
NoteHint 1
  1. curve’s formula, plus any columns you think matter. Call it super.
  2. AIC() of curve and super.
NoteHint 2 
names(elec)
super <- lm(gas_twh ~ poly(year, 4) + ______ + ______, data = elec)
summary(super)
AIC(curve, super)
TipSolution 
names(elec)
super <- lm(gas_twh ~ poly(year, 4) + coal_twh + wind_twh + gas_price,
            data = elec)
summary(super)
AIC(curve, super)

AIC 234.9 for the curve, 197.2 with coal, wind and the gas price. One at a time, coal does nearly all the work: −0.95 TWh of gas for each TWh of coal, R² 0.55 → 0.85. The 2012–15 trough was coal: cheap coal and dear gas.

The gas price comes out positive: dearer gas, more gas burnt? Added alone it was −0.8; with coal in the model the sign flips. A coefficient depends on the company it keeps, and is not a cause.

10. Let R choose: step()

Slide

  • step(model) (new): starts from the model you give it and drops one term at a time while that lowers the AIC. It prints each step.
  • formula(model) (new): gives the formula a model was fitted with.
  • Already met: lm(), AIC(), summary().
NoteHint 1
  1. Fit everything: the curve plus every other column. In a formula, . means “every column except the outcome”, year included, so gas_twh ~ poly(year, 4) + . - year saves typing them out. Hand it to step() and call the result chosen.
  2. Its formula, its summary, and AIC() against curve.
NoteHint 2 
everything <- lm(gas_twh ~ poly(year, 4) + . - year, data = elec)
chosen <- step(______)
formula(______)
summary(chosen)
AIC(curve, chosen)
TipSolution 
everything <- lm(gas_twh ~ poly(year, 4) + . - year, data = elec)
chosen <- step(everything)
formula(chosen)
summary(chosen)
AIC(curve, chosen)

step() drops the curve first (all four terms at once: it can drop poly(year, 4), not choose its degree), then carbon_floor. It keeps the other fuels, demand, gas price, temperature, imports, GDP and COVID: AIC 92.9, R² 0.999.

Read the coefficients: about −1 for each other fuel, about +1 for demand. Gas is whatever demand the other fuels leave. That is the electricity balance sheet, not an explanation: why did coal go? step() can only choose from what you gave it.

The AIC in step()’s printout is on a different scale from AIC()’s; only differences matter.
