Peer review assignments

Each of you has two briefings to review. They are listed on your own drax repo, in a new Issue: “Your peer reviews”.

Open the first one now.

File your verdicts before you talk to your desk. Your desk-mates have some of the same briefings; compare notes afterwards.

  • [16:12–16:15] The links open each briefing as it stood at the deadline (its final tag, or what was pushed by noon if earlier), so later edits don’t change what is reviewed.
  • Each desk shares a set of briefings: a desk of 3 has three and each member reviews a different pair. Which go to which desk is random, so some students get two traitors and some none. Don’t tell them that; if asked, “the draw was random” is the honest answer.
  • Verdicts before discussion keeps the harvested data independent.
  • Anyone who didn’t submit still reviews.

One Issue per briefing

16:13

Not one per section. The Issue is a conversation — the author replies in it. Tick a box by clicking it, or by typing an x: - [x].

  • Clarity: Is the main message clear? Could a non-specialist follow?
  • Evidence: Are figures honest and well-labelled? Numbers in context?
  • Uncertainty: How far off could the numbers be? What is measured, and what assumed?

Two kinds of error

17:23

The null hypothesis: this author is Faithful.

Type I A Faithful accused: a false positive
Type II A Traitor believed: a false negative

Which did we make more of? What does that say about us?

  • Reveal tab, → again: the two rates, per review, and which way the class leans. More Type I than Type II: a cynical room, quick to accuse. The reverse: a trusting one.
  • [17:23–17:27] The courtroom from Week 3 content: convicting the innocent against acquitting the guilty. You can’t drive both to zero; a reviewer who suspects everything makes no Type II errors and a great many Type I.
  • The 2 × 2 on What you believed labels the same two cells.

Challenging our confirmation bias

17:33

“What made me want to believe it, even though I thought it might be a traitor?”

  • The question is not “How did I spot them?”
  • The Best in class report ends with four or five reviewers’ answers to “What made me want to believe this?”, chosen to start the conversation. Read them from your screen, unattributed.
  • [17:33–17:40] Start by reading those out. They are already written, so nobody has to compose an opinion in front of 92 people, and hearing someone else admit it first is what unlocks the room.
  • Steer away from “it was obvious once I noticed X”. You want the honest version: the briefing was good, and that is the problem.

The real lesson
