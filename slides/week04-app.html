Briefing structure reminder

  1. Summary — the answer, up front
  2. Evidence — figures from Weeks 2–3, with captions
  3. Caveats — what depends on assumptions
  4. Conclusions — your recommendation
  • They can write in .qmd, .Rmd, .md, or even Word with exported figures. The format matters less than the content.

Phase 1: Write

✏️ 45 minutes

Writing block

You have until the clock says 50 minutes.

  • Reuse and refine figures from Weeks 2–3
  • The analytical work is done — this is about synthesis
  • Traitors: be credible, not cartoonish

Hard deadline: whatever is committed at minute 50 gets reviewed.

  • [45 min] Circulate for questions about content, structure, and R code. This is the main working block.
  • Traitors may need reassurance: “You’re not lying. You’re writing a technically sourced briefing that happens to cherry-pick.”

Phase 2: Review

✏️ 30 minutes

Peer review assignments

Each of you has two repos to review.

Navigate to the first one on GitHub now.

  • [5 min setup] Hand out assignments. Two each, drawn at random — so some of you will get two traitors and some none. Don’t tell them that; if asked, “the draw was random” is the honest answer.

One Issue per briefing

Not one per section. The Issue is a conversation — the author replies in it and ticks the boxes off as they act on you.

  • Clarity: Is the main message clear? Could a non-specialist follow?
  • Evidence: Are figures honest and well-labelled? Numbers in context?
  • Uncertainty: Are limitations acknowledged?

Start with these three lines

Reviewer: your-username
Verdict: FAITHFUL          (or SUSPECT)
Argues: CARBON-NEUTRAL     (or NOT-CARBON-NEUTRAL / MIXED)

Exactly as written. A script reads them — this is the vote.

  • Say it plainly: there is no separate poll later. If the header lines are mangled the review still helps the author but vanishes from the class results.

Then two required paragraphs

Why I reached that verdict — point at a figure, a claim, an omission. Not “it feels wrong”.

What made me want to believe this — answer it even if you voted SUSPECT.

  • [30 min] Quiet, focused work. Circulate, and push hardest on the second paragraph — students skip it, and it is the raw material for the debrief.
  • Warn them their answers may be read out, unattributed.
  • At minute 80, run the harvester for the first time and keep the output. It is your fallback if the API stalls during the reveal.

Phase 3: The reveal

💬🎓 20 minutes

Nobody stands up

There are 93 of us. You don’t know whose briefing you read, and they don’t know you read it.

So instead: the class reads its own data.

Every verdict you filed in the last half hour is already counted.

  • Say this out loud early — it defuses the anxiety that has been sitting under the writing block all session.
  • No traitor is named, tonight or ever. Anyone who wants the credit can claim it themselves afterwards.

What you believed

Actually faithful Actually traitor
Voted FAITHFUL correct fooled
Voted SUSPECT wrongly accused caught
  • [3 min] Read the two off-diagonal cells first — they are the interesting ones.
  • “Wrongly accused” is usually bigger than students expect, and it is the honest briefings that looked deceptive. Flag it; you come back to it in two slides.

The split that matters

Suspicion rate, broken down by what the briefing argued.

Were you tougher on briefings that disagreed with you?

  • [3 min] This is the payoff for making Argues a separate field.
  • Traitors were explicitly allowed to argue the popular conclusion by dishonest means. If those slipped through at a higher rate than traitors arguing the unpopular one, that is confirmation bias with the class’s own name on it.
  • Don’t soften it, and don’t blame anyone: every reviewer in the room did the same thing.

The three that got away

The least-suspected traitor briefings. Anonymised.

Which techniques survived scrutiny?

  • [5 min] Pick one and dissect it against the teaching notes — the techniques you held back from the content session.
  • Credit the craft. These students did a hard job well.

And the one you all accused

An honest briefing. Most-flagged in the class.

What did it do that looked like deception?

  • [3 min] Usually: a missing comparator, an unlabelled axis, a confident sentence with no caveat. Nothing deliberate.
  • This is the richest slide in the deck. Honest mistakes and deliberate deception are indistinguishable from the outside.

The question

Not “how did you spot them?”

“What made me want to believe it — even though I thought it might be a traitor?”

  • [6 min] Start by reading out four or five harvested answers. They are already written, so nobody has to compose an opinion in front of 92 people, and hearing someone else admit it first is what unlocks the room.
  • Steer away from “it was obvious once I noticed X”. You want the honest version: the briefing was good, and that is the problem.

The real lesson

Honest mistakes and deliberate deception can look identical.

The skills that help you spot a traitor also help you avoid being one by accident.
