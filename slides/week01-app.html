A first plot

“What’s the first thing you notice?”

  • total gen > sum(sources) because of unlisted “other”

  • Again, type it. Put the bare plot() up first, with no labels, and ask the room what is wrong with it before adding them.

  • Total generation, not bioenergy, so the students still have Exercise 2 to do themselves; the same plot() pattern carries over.

  • Anticipated code:

    plot(elec$year, elec$total_twh,
     xlab = "Year", ylab = "Total generation (TWh)",
     main = "UK electricity generation")

  • elec comes from the previous slide’s cell — run that one first, or re-read the CSV here.

  • Total generation peaked at 398 TWh in 2005 and fell to 294 TWh in 2025, down 26 %. Most students expect it to have grown.

  • Ask: “How does this compare to what you expected?” Then: “why might it have fallen?” (efficiency, less heavy industry, more imports).

  • Plant the seed for Exercise 5: any fuel’s share rises when the total falls, even if the fuel itself stands still. “Is that a big number?” comes next week.

  • Drax converted its first unit to biomass in 2013 and stopped burning coal in 2021; the UK’s last coal plant (Ratcliffe) closed in September 2024, so 2024 still shows 2 TWh of coal; DUKES 2026 leaves 2025 blank, which the course file records as 0.

Git

📋🎓💻 Setting up your repo

Why Git?

Your code needs to be:

  • Saved — not just in the browser
  • Versioned — so you can go back if something breaks
  • Visible — so you can share your progress

Git does all three. GitHub Desktop makes it painless.

  • [16:55–17:00] Brief motivation. Don’t oversell Git — just frame it as “how professionals save their work.”

The workflow

Every session:

  1. Open GitHub Desktop
  2. Pull any changes
  3. Do your work in .R files
  4. Commit with a message
  5. Push

That’s it.

Your commit history becomes your learning record.

Committing loads the van; pushing sends it to the depot.

  • Emphasize simplicity. Resist the urge to explain branches, merges, or anything advanced. Week 5 will cover collaboration.+

  • Committing is like loading your changes onto a removals van on your driveway.

  • Pushing sends the van to the depot – your changes are safe

  • Pulling delivers any changes from the depot to wherever you are working.

Coal out, bioenergy in

w01-coal-and-bioenergy.R

  • This is the chart from Exercise 3, drawn the same way: plot(), then lines(), then legend(), bioenergy solid and coal dashed. Say so — students should recognize their own code, not a lecturer’s better version of it.
  • The headline has a silent “some”: coal lost about 120 TWh, bioenergy gained about 37, and gas and wind filled the rest.
