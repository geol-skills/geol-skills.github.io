What is a model?

11:08

A deliberate simplification that captures some features of reality and ignores others.

All statistical tests include models: the t-test models the data as two normal distributions, one per group.

Today we make the model explicit, and build one up live:

\[y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \varepsilon\]

“y changes by β₁ for every one-unit change in x, plus noise.”

(aka \(y = mx + c\): Linear regression)

  • [11:08–11:35] Annual gas, live: a straight line, its null world, its summary, its diagnostics, its uncertainty, then a curve. Students watch; they practise on the exercise page afterwards.
  • The question for the whole session: how much UK electricity comes from gas, and what drives it?

How much of our electricity comes from gas?

11:11

  • Anticipated code:

    ggplot(elec, aes(x = year, y = gas_twh)) +
  geom_point()

model <- lm(gas_twh ~ year, data = elec)
coef(model)

  • Before fitting, ask: is gas going up or down? Most will say down.

  • Slope −2.34 TWh a year. Intercept 4836 TWh: the line’s prediction for the year 0. Meaningless: it is where the line crosses x = 0, far outside the data.

  • lm() uses the same outcome ~ predictor formula as t.test().

Could chance draw this line?

11:14

  • [5 min, within 11:08–11:35] Ask first: “Could a line this steep turn up by chance?” Then: “How did we answer that in Week 6?” Same five steps; only the number changes, from δ to the slope.

  • Anticipated code:

    slope <- coef(model)[["year"]]
slope

shuffled <- sample(elec$gas_twh)
coef(lm(shuffled ~ elec$year))[[2]]

set.seed(2341)
null_slopes <- replicate(1000, {
  coef(lm(sample(gas_twh) ~ year, data = elec))[["year"]]
})

ggplot(data.frame(null_slopes), aes(x = null_slopes)) +
  geom_histogram(bins = 30) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = c(slope, -slope), colour = "red")

mean(abs(null_slopes) >= abs(slope))

  • Step 2 unseeded: run it two or three times; each shuffle gives a slope near zero.

  • Numbers from a real run: slope −2.34 TWh a year. Null slopes run from −2.0 to +2.6; 1 of 1000 is as steep (+2.6), so p ≈ 0.001. With 10 000 shuffles, 2 in 10 000: p = 0.0002.

  • Shuffling treats the years as interchangeable. Two slides on, the diagnostics question that: neighbouring years are alike.

  • Next slide: summary() prints the same p-value without shuffling. That column is the shortcut, as t.test() was in Week 6.

What else does R know about the line?

11:17
  • Anticipated code: summary(model)
  • Read it off together:
    • Slope −2.34 TWh a year, standard error 0.53: t = −4.38, p = 0.0002: the null world’s answer, from a formula. Chance rarely draws a line this steep.
    • R² = 0.44: the line accounts for 44 % of the year-to-year variation. 56 % is something else.
    • Residual standard error 20.4 TWh on 24 degrees of freedom (26 years, minus two coefficients): a typical point sits 20 TWh off the line.
  • “Is that a big number?” Gas is about 130 TWh in a typical year; 20 TWh is a sixth of it.
  • The exercise page (Exercise 2) has the same table to work through.

Does a straight line fit?

11:20

  • Anticipated code, one plot per Run:

    plot(model, which = 1)

    Read it, then change the 1 to a 2 and run again:

    plot(model, which = 2)

    plot(model) alone gives four plots, one after another; which picks one. One at a time, each gets a square panel: squashed side by side, the residual pattern is hard to see.

  • Residuals vs fitted is not a cloud. Residuals run positive in 2007–10 (+20 to +37 TWh), then sharply negative in 2012–15 (−27 to −36), then positive again. Neighbouring years are alike: the residuals are not independent.

  • The four assumptions, if asked: linearity, independence, constant variance, normal residuals (only needed for p-values and intervals).

  • What happened in 2012–15? Cheap coal and expensive gas: generators switched to coal until the carbon price floor made coal uneconomic in

    1. Time is not the cause of anything; the model has nothing else to go on.

A curve?

11:26

Polished version: w07-gas-annual.R

  • Anticipated code:

    head(poly(elec$year, 4))   # four columns, one row per year

curve <- lm(gas_twh ~ poly(year, 4), data = elec)
summary(curve)

ggplot(elec, aes(x = year, y = gas_twh)) +
  geom_point() +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm", formula = y ~ poly(x, 4))

  • Why not just add year², year³, year⁴? Over 2000–2025 they are almost the same variable (year and year² correlate at 0.999 999). Try lm(gas_twh ~ year + I(year^2) + I(year^3) + I(year^4)): the year³ coefficient comes back NA, dropped without a warning. poly() rebuilds the four columns so they don’t overlap.

  • The price: the coefficients no longer mean anything on their own. The first, −89.4, is not “TWh per year”; the slope is still −2.34.

  • R² 0.55 (the line: 0.44). The curve rises to 2004, sags through 2012–15, and dives after 2020, chasing the last few points.

Is the curve better?

11:29

Which do you believe? What is the model not capturing?

  • Anticipated code:

    summary(model)$r.squared   # 0.444
summary(curve)$r.squared   # 0.552

future <- data.frame(year = c(2030, 2040))
predict(model, future)     # 92.1, 68.7 TWh
predict(curve, future)     # −67.9, −1356 TWh

  • R² always rises when you add terms, even useless ones. Is 0.44 → 0.55 a big number? Hold that thought: AIC answers it after the exercises.

  • Extrapolated, the curve takes gas below zero in 2028; the line still has 69 TWh in 2040. Same data, very different futures. Neither model knows about gas prices, carbon prices or new nuclear: blind spots.

What’s wrong?

11:48

w07-residuals.R

  • The residuals show a seasonal pattern: the model is missing a time-dependent structure
  • R² = 0.49 means 51 % of the variation is unexplained
  • “Dominant” is a stretch when your model misses more than it captures

A fair comparison: AIC

12:00

\[\mathrm{AIC} = 2k - 2 \ln \hat{L}\]

k coefficients; L̂: how likely the data are under the fitted model.

  • [12:00–12:45] Where we left off: the line misses a trough in 2012–15 (its residuals have a shape), and the curve explains more, but R² always rises. AIC charges 2 for every coefficient.

  • Anticipated code:

    AIC(model, curve)
AIC(lm(gas_twh ~ poly(year, 2), data = elec))

    then change the 2, a degree at a time, as far as the room wants.

    Degree 1 2 3 4 5 6 8 10 12
    AIC 234.5 235.3 236.7 234.9 234.2 230.3 225.8 209.7 201.4
    R² 0.44 0.47 0.48 0.55 0.60 0.68 0.77 0.89 0.93

  • Degrees 1 to 5 are within 2.5 of each other: a tie. AIC differences under about 2 mean nothing; above about 10 they are decisive. The curve’s extra R² was not worth its three extra terms.

  • Push on and AIC keeps falling: 13 coefficients for 26 years at degree

    1. AIC’s penalty is gentle with so few points, but the bigger message is that the wiggles are catching something real: the 2012–15 trough. A year can’t cause a trough. What can?

  • If the room asks “so which degree?”: AIC can’t separate 1 to 5, and beyond that it is rewarding the curve for imitating a cause it doesn’t contain. Carry the curve forward and find the cause.

  • AIC compares models of the same data only.

Did the extra terms help?

12:18

  • Anticipated code, with whatever the room named:

    names(elec)
super <- lm(gas_twh ~ poly(year, 4) + coal_twh + wind_twh + gas_price,
            data = elec)
summary(super)
AIC(curve, super)

  • Each suggestion on its own, added to curve (AIC 234.9, R² 0.55):

    Added AIC Added AIC
    coal_twh 207.9 wind_twh 234.1
    total_twh 231.4 solar_twh 235.4
    nuclear_twh 232.1 temp_c 235.5
    carbon_floor 232.3 gdp 236.0
    imports_twh 233.5 covid 236.8
    gas_price, bioenergy_twh 236.9

  • Coal is the one that matters: −0.95 TWh of gas for each TWh of coal, R² 0.55 → 0.85, AIC down 27. The 2012–15 trough was coal: cheap coal and dear gas. Most of the others don’t earn their 2.

  • The example above: AIC 197.2, R² 0.92. gas_price comes out positive, +8.2 TWh per p/kWh: dearer gas, more gas burnt? Is that plausible? Alone, beside the curve, it was −0.8; with coal in the model the sign flips. A coefficient depends on the company it keeps, and is not a cause.

  • If the room names demand and every other fuel, R² reaches 0.998 and AIC falls below 100, with coefficients near −1 and +1: gas is demand minus everything else. Bookkeeping, not explanation: the next slide.

Let R choose: step()

12:27

step() can only choose from what you gave it.

  • Anticipated code:

    everything <- lm(gas_twh ~ poly(year, 4) + . - year, data = elec)
chosen <- step(everything)
formula(chosen)
summary(chosen)
AIC(curve, chosen)

    In a formula, . is every column except the outcome, year included; - year takes plain year back out, leaving the curve.

  • everything: 17 coefficients, R² 0.999. step() drops poly(year, 4) first, all four terms at once (it can drop the curve, not choose its degree), then carbon_floor. It keeps the other five fuels, demand, gas price, temperature, imports, GDP and COVID: 12 coefficients, AIC 92.9 against the curve’s 234.9.

  • Read the coefficients: coal −0.95, nuclear −0.86, wind −0.99, solar −0.91, biomass −1.18, demand +0.88. Gas is whatever demand the other fuels leave. Time was never the cause; but this is the electricity balance sheet, not an explanation. Why did coal go? Prices, policy, closures: none of which step() was offered.

  • The AIC in step()’s printout (21 → 17) is on a different scale from AIC()’s; only differences matter.

  • Fitting dozens of models and keeping the best is a multiple- comparisons trap: try enough, and one wins by chance (Week 8 returns to this). Test the winner on data it didn’t choose from: the worksheet’s last extension does, on daily data.

What is the model not capturing?

12:36

Overfitting: too much faith in your data (a degree-12 curve through 26 years).

Extrapolation: too much faith in your model (no gas by 2028).

Blind spots: too much faith in your framework (step() chose from our list, and only ours).

  • Three forms of the same mistake: confusing the map for the territory.

Key points

12:50
  1. A model is a simplification: lm(outcome ~ a + b)
  2. A slope’s p-value: how often a null world draws a line this steep
  3. Check the diagnostics: residuals don’t lie
  4. R² always rises; AIC charges for each coefficient
  5. step() can only choose from what you gave it
  6. Always ask: “What is my model not capturing?”
