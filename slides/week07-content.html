What is a model?

11:08

A deliberate simplification that captures some features of reality and ignores others.

All statistical tests include models: the t-test models the data as two normal distributions, one per group.

Today we build explicit models, starting with linear regression:

\[y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \varepsilon\]

“y changes by β₁ for every one-unit change in x, plus noise.”

(aka \(y = mx + c\))

  • [11:08–11:35] Annual gas, live: a straight line, its null world, its summary, its diagnostics, its uncertainty, then a curve. Students watch; they practise on the exercise page afterwards.
  • The question for the whole session: how much UK electricity comes from gas, and what drives it?

How much of our electricity comes from gas?

11:11

  • Anticipated code:

    ggplot(elec, aes(x = year, y = gas_twh)) +

model <- lm(gas_twh ~ year, data = elec)
coef(model)

  • Before fitting, ask: is gas going up or down?

  • Slope −2.34 TWh a year. Intercept 4836 TWh: the line’s prediction for the year 0. Meaningless: it is where the line crosses x = 0, far outside the data.

  • lm() uses the same outcome ~ predictor formula as t.test().

Could chance draw this line?

11:15

Black hole: BoliviaInteligente / Unsplash

  • [5 min, within 11:08–11:35] Ask first: “Could a line this steep turn up by chance?” Then: “How did we answer that in Week 6?” Same five steps; only the number changes, from δ to the slope.

  • Anticipated code:

    slope <- coef(model)[["year"]]
slope

shuffled <- sample(elec$gas_twh)
coef(lm(shuffled ~ elec$year))[[2]]

set.seed(2341)
null_slopes <- replicate(100, { # 1000 is slow
  coef(lm(sample(gas_twh) ~ year, data = elec))[["year"]]
})

ggplot(data.frame(null_slopes), aes(x = null_slopes)) +
  geom_histogram(bins = 30) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = c(slope, -slope), colour = "red")

mean(abs(null_slopes) >= abs(slope))

  • Step 2 unseeded: run it two or three times; each shuffle gives a slope near zero.

  • Numbers from a real run: slope −2.34 TWh a year. Null slopes run from −2.0 to +2.6; 1 of 1000 is as steep (+2.6), so p ≈ 0.001. With 10 000 shuffles, 2 in 10 000: p = 0.0002.

  • Shuffling treats the years as interchangeable. Two slides on, the diagnostics question that: neighbouring years are alike.

  • Next slide: summary() prints the same p-value without shuffling. That column is the shortcut, as t.test() was in Week 6.

What else does R know about the line?

11:18
  • Anticipated code: summary(model)
  • Read it off together:
    • Slope −2.34 TWh a year, standard error 0.53: t = −4.38, p = 0.0002: the null world’s answer, from a formula. Chance rarely draws a line this steep.
    • R² = 0.44: the line accounts for 44 % of the year-to-year variation. 56 % is something else.
    • Residual standard error 20.4 TWh on 24 degrees of freedom (26 years, minus two coefficients): a typical point sits 20 TWh off the line.
  • “Is that a big number?” Gas is about 130 TWh in a typical year; 20 TWh is a sixth of it.
  • The exercise page (Exercise 2) has the same table to work through.

Does a straight line fit?

11:22

  • Anticipated code, one plot per Run:

    plot(model, which = 1)

    Read it, then change the 1 to a 2 and run again:

    plot(model, which = 2)

  • Residuals vs fitted is not a cloud. Residuals run positive in 2007–10 (+20 to +37 TWh), then sharply negative in 2012–15 (−27 to −36), then positive again. Neighbouring years are alike: the residuals are not independent.

  • QQ plot: S-shape means more big divergences than normal distribution (1:1 line) predicts.

  • The four assumptions, if asked: linearity, independence, constant variance, normal residuals (only needed for p-values and intervals).

  • What happened in 2012–15? Cheap coal and expensive gas: generators switched to coal until the carbon price floor made coal uneconomic in

    1. Time is not the cause of anything; the model has nothing else to go on.

Ask about 2026 both ways: predict(model, data.frame(year = 2026), interval = "confidence") gives 101 TWh (84–118); interval = "prediction" gives 101 (56–147): it’s likely that the value will fall outside the band, as most points do!

A curve?

11:28

Polished version: w07-gas-annual.R

  • Anticipated code:

    head(poly(elec$year, 4))   # four columns, one row per year

curve <- lm(gas_twh ~ poly(year, 4), data = elec)
summary(curve)

ggplot(elec, aes(x = year, y = gas_twh)) +
  geom_point() +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm", formula = y ~ poly(x, 4))

  • After first pass, increase the polynomial coeff to show the curve warping to the data. 10 gives lots of stat sig fits!

  • Why not just add year², year³, year⁴? Over 2000–2025 they are almost the same variable (year and year² correlate at 0.999 999). Try lm(gas_twh ~ year + I(year^2) + I(year^3) + I(year^4)): the year³ coefficient comes back NA, dropped without a warning. poly() rebuilds the four columns so they don’t overlap.

  • The price: the coefficients no longer mean anything on their own. The first, −89.4, is not “TWh per year”; the slope is still −2.34.

  • R² 0.55 (the line: 0.44). The curve rises to 2004, sags through 2012–15, and dives after 2020, chasing the last few points.

Is the curve better?

11:32

  • Anticipated code:

    summary(model)$r.squared   # 0.444
summary(curve)$r.squared   # 0.552

future <- data.frame(year = c(2030, 2040))
predict(model, future)     # 92.1, 68.7 TWh
predict(curve, future)     # −67.9, −1356 TWh

  • R² always rises when you add terms, even useless ones. Is 0.44 → 0.55 a big number? Hold that thought: AIC answers it after the exercises (it is a tie).

  • Extrapolated, the curve takes gas below zero in 2028; the line still has 69 TWh in 2040. Same data, very different futures. Neither model knows about gas prices, carbon prices or new nuclear: blind spots.

Which do you believe? What does the model miss?

A fair comparison: AIC

12:00

\[\mathrm{AIC} = 2k - 2 \ln \hat{L}\]

k: parameters estimated; L̂: how likely the data are under the fitted model.

  • [12:00–12:45] Straight on from the residuals: they have a trend and a cycle. Adding terms always raises R², so is each one worth it? AIC charges 2 for every parameter (R also counts the residual spread, so HolmesCo’s line has k = 3).

  • Anticipated code:

    trend <- lm(gw_level_m ~ rainfall_mm + month, data = groundwater)
AIC(holmes, trend)
AIC(lm(gw_level_m ~ rainfall_mm + poly(month, 2), data = groundwater))
groundwater$sin <- sin(2 * pi * groundwater$month / 12)
groundwater$cos <- cos(2 * pi * groundwater$month / 12)
season <- lm(gw_level_m ~ rainfall_mm + month + sin + cos,
             data = groundwater)
AIC(trend, season)
both <- lm(gw_level_m ~ rainfall_mm * month + sin + cos,
           data = groundwater)
AIC(season, both)
    Model Coefficients AIC R²
    holmes: rainfall 2 181.4 0.49
    trend: + month 3 177.8 0.53
    + poly(month, 2) 4 179.7 0.53
    + poly(month, 4) 6 182.6 0.54
    + poly(month, 8) 10 174.4 0.65
    + poly(month, 12) 14 163.9 0.74
    season: + month + sin + cos 5 100.2 0.88
    both: rainfall_mm * month + sin + cos 6 102.2 0.88

  • AIC differences under about 2 mean nothing; above about 10 they are decisive. The trend earns its place (−3.6). Degrees 2–4 raise AIC: the extra terms don’t pay for themselves. Twelve degrees of wiggle buy 14 points; four terms with the right shape buy 78. What matters is choosing the right terms, not adding more.

  • The sine–cosine pair: together they can make a yearly wave peaking in any month (Exercise 4 on the worksheet). Naming the columns sin and cos keeps formulas and step()’s printout short; R still finds the functions sin() and cos() when you call them.

  • Interactions. a * b in a formula is a + b + a:b: a:b lets the effect of a change as b changes. Here: does each mm of rain raise the water table more, or less, as the years pass? Its coefficient is 0.0000 (p = 0.97), and AIC rises by 2.0, the price of one useless term. The simulation had no interaction; AIC agrees.

  • AIC compares models of the same data only: never groundwater against gas.

  • Bridge: the season and the trend were causes we could name. Back to gas: what would we name there? (Line against curve for gas: AIC 234.5 against 234.9, a tie.)

Let R choose: step()

12:09

  • Anticipated code:

    everything <- lm(gw_level_m ~ (rainfall_mm + month + sin + cos)^2,
                 data = groundwater)
chosen <- step(everything) # then add `, trace = 0`
formula(chosen)
summary(chosen)

step(lm(gw_level_m ~ rainfall_mm * month, data = groundwater))

    (a + b + c)^2 is every term, plus every pair’s interaction: 4 + 6 terms, 11 coefficients.

  • Read the printout one table at a time: each row is “drop this term”; <none> is “drop nothing”. step() takes the lowest row, refits, and repeats. Six steps, one interaction each: month:sin, rainfall_mm:cos, rainfall_mm:month, sin:cos, rainfall_mm:sin, month:cos. Then <none> is top: dropping any of the four would cost AIC (cos most, by 77).

  • It never offers to drop sin while an interaction still uses it.

  • It lands on rainfall_mm + month + sin + cos: how the data were made. Rain 0.057 m per mm (simulated: 0.07); trend −0.017 m a month (−0.015). We can only check that because the data are simulated.

  • Without the seasonal columns, step() keeps rainfall_mm + month (AIC 177.8 against 100.2), and never finds the seasons. HolmesCo’s mistake, automated.

  • The AIC in step()’s printout (−62.4 → −72.1) is AIC() minus a constant (here 172.3) that depends only on the number of observations. Only differences matter.

  • Fitting dozens of models and keeping the best is a multiple- comparisons trap: try enough, and one wins by chance (Week 8 returns to this).

step() can only choose from what you gave it.

Autocorrelation

12:18

Drax power station: © Drax

  • Recall “Could chance draw this line?”: shuffling treated the years as interchangeable. Are they?

  • Anticipated code:

    bio <- lm(bioenergy_twh ~ year, data = elec)
summary(bio)

elec$resid <- residuals(bio)
ggplot(elec, aes(x = year, y = resid)) +
  geom_col()

r <- elec$resid
cor(head(r, -1), tail(r, -1))

set.seed(2341)
null_cor <- replicate(1000, {
  shuffled <- sample(r)
  cor(head(shuffled, -1), tail(shuffled, -1))
})
range(null_cor)
mean(abs(null_cor) >= 0.82)

  • Introduce head(r, -1) (every year but the last).

  • R² 0.93, slope 1.69 TWh a year, p = 4 × 10⁻¹⁵. Looks conclusive.

  • The residuals come in four runs: above the line 2000–05, below 2006–14, above 2015–21, below 2022–25. A shuffle would give about 14.

  • Correlation of each year’s residual with the next: 0.82. Shuffled, −0.56 to 0.61; none of 1000 reach 0.82.

  • Why? Electricity from biomass depends on power stations, and these take years to build or convert. Drax converted one unit at a time: its biomass output went 1.4 → 3.1 → 7.9 → 11.5 → 12.7 TWh over 2012–16, most of the UK’s rise from 15 to 30 TWh.

  • This is autocorrelation: neighbouring observations are alike. The p-value assumes 26 independent years; 26 years this alike carry about as much information as 3. The trend is real, but the model is too sure of itself.

Part-whole correlation

12:27

Flaring gas: Jozef G., Pexels

  • Anticipated code:

    demand <- lm(gas_twh ~ total_twh, data = elec)
summary(demand)$r.squared

elec$not_gas <- elec$total_twh - elec$gas_twh
set.seed(2341)
null_r2 <- replicate(100, {
  fake_total <- elec$gas_twh + sample(elec$not_gas)
  summary(lm(elec$gas_twh ~ fake_total))$r.squared
})
ggplot(data.frame(null_r2), aes(x = null_r2)) +
  geom_histogram(bins = 30) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = 0.57, colour = "red")
mean(null_r2 >= 0.57)

rest <- lm(gas_twh ~ not_gas, data = elec)
summary(rest)$r.squared

  • Gas against total: R² 0.57. Is that a big number? What is wrong with this model?

  • Gas is part of the total: the model partly predicts gas from gas. Shuffled, where gas has nothing to do with the rest, R² still runs from 0.35 to 0.82 (median 0.59); 54 of 100 shuffles beat 0.57. This is part–whole correlation: all of the 0.57 comes from gas being inside the total.

  • The honest question, gas against everything else: R² 0.001 (p = 0.87). Coal’s fall was made up by wind and solar; the rest of the fleet stayed between 185 and 264 TWh, and gas fell with demand.

  • Watch for it in projects: a part against its whole, or two ratios that share a denominator.

What is the model not capturing?

12:36

The map, not the territory: William Smith, 1815 (public domain)

Overfitting: too much faith in your data (a degree-4 curve through 26 years).

Extrapolation: too much faith in your model (no gas by 2028).

Autocorrelation: too much faith in your sample size (26 years of biomass that carry the information of 3).

Blind spots: too much faith in your framework (step() can choose only from its ingredients).

  • Four forms of the same mistake: confusing the map for the territory.

Key points

12:50
  1. A model is a simplification: lm(outcome ~ a + b)
  2. A slope’s p-value: how often a null world draws a line this steep
  3. Check the diagnostics: residuals don’t lie
  4. Autocorrelation: non-independent data give less information than you think
  5. Adding coefficients improves R²; AIC asks whether they earn their keep
  6. step() can only choose from what you gave it
  7. Always ask: “What is my model not capturing?”
