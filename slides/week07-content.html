The problem

Three groups (e.g., wind farms in three regions).

You could run three pairwise t-tests: A vs B, A vs C, B vs C.

Each test, on its own, has a 5 % chance of a false positive.

Run 20 tests where nothing is going on. How often do you “find” something?

  • [11:10–11:30] Multiple comparisons. Take guesses before the next slide; most of the room will say 5 % or “once”.

Chance in action

  • Type this, don’t paste it. Same move as Week 6’s null world: build a world where nothing is going on, then watch what the test does there.

  • Anticipated code:

    t.test(rnorm(25), rnorm(25))$p.value

survey <- replicate(20, t.test(rnorm(25), rnorm(25))$p.value)
sum(survey < 0.05)

set.seed(7)
hits <- replicate(1000, {
  survey <- replicate(20, t.test(rnorm(25), rnorm(25))$p.value)
  sum(survey < 0.05)
})
mean(hits >= 1)
table(hits)

  • Run steps 1 and 2 unseeded, three or four times each: the room sees p bounce around, and some surveys turn up one or two “discoveries”.

  • Step 3, from a real run with set.seed(7) just before replicate(): 67 % of surveys find at least one; 76 of 1000 (about 1 in 13) find three or more. Theory says 64 %; the difference is simulation noise.

  • Then the shortcut, exactly as the t-test was a shortcut for the shuffle: 1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ 0.64. Put the formula on the board; it reappears in Exercise 1.

HolmesCo’s mineral survey

⚒️ HolmesCo

Geological Consultants Since 2019

“Leave No Stone Untested”™

Press Release — Multi-Element Anomaly Discovered!

“We tested soil samples from 20 sites for enrichment in 20 elements. We found statistically significant enrichment (p < 0.05) for three elements at one site!”

If there’s truly nothing there, how many significant results would you expect by chance?

  • Their survey is 20 of our simulated surveys, one per site. Students can reason from the last slide: 20 expected false positives, about one per site.

The answer

20 × 20 = 400 tests at α = 0.05

Expected false positives: 20, about one per site.

With nothing there at all, some site scores three or more four times in five.

The “discovery” is what noise looks like.

  • Each site’s hits are binomial(20, 0.05): one site reaches three with probability 0.075; across 20 sites, 1 − 0.925²⁰ ≈ 0.79.
  • In the simulation, about 1 survey in 13 found three or more by itself. With 20 sites, one of them doing so is the expected outcome.
  • Ask what the press release leaves out: the total number of significant results across all 400 tests, and which site HolmesCo would have predicted before looking. They picked the site after seeing which came out best.

Your project

How many comparisons are you planning to make?

If you run all of them at α = 0.05, what’s your family-wise false positive rate?

Formula: 1 − (1 − α)k where k = number of tests

Same data, four ways

Before any test: how you show a comparison changes what you see.

Four regions, same 100 sites, same order (low mean to high) — shown four different ways.

  • [11:37–11:42, inside concept block 2] A quick sequence, no discussion prompts needed on each panel — let the four builds speak for themselves, then land the takeaway on the last one.
  • Idea adapted from Andrew Heiss, Data Visualization with R, session 6 (“Uncertainty”) and session 8 (“Comparisons”) — After Heiss (2026).
  • Timing note: this concept block grew from 20 to 25 minutes to fit the sequence; the preceding “How many tests?” exercise and the “Run an ANOVA” exercise after it were each trimmed to compensate. See sessions/weeks-5-10.md.

Same data, four ways: the bar

w07-groups-bar.R

  • The “dynamite plot.” Ask: would you trust this? What would change your mind?
  • What it hides: the spread within each region, the sample size, and whether the gap between any two bars is bigger than the noise.
  • Region order here is by group mean. The exercise page’s Exercise 1 orders its violins by median, which swaps Wales and Southern England — worth a one-line heads-up if a student notices the mismatch.

Same data, four ways: the box plot

w07-groups-box.R

  • Better: the spread is visible, and the two-cluster pattern starts to show. Still hides the individual sites and the sample size per box.
  • If someone asks “why not just use this” — flag Nick Desbarats’ Nightingale piece, linked on the exercise page: box plots take longer to teach than most students realize, and a good fraction of any room cannot read one confidently.

Same data, four ways: the raw points

w07-groups-points.R

Takeaway: a bar of means can hide a bad idea; the points behind it usually don’t.

  • This is the picture ANOVA reasons about: a mean, its uncertainty, and the scatter behind it. Compare it to the F-ratio figure three slides later — same encoding, cyan points and an ink summary.
  • Point out that individual sites overlap heavily between regions even though the means and CIs are cleanly separated. Both things are true at once.

Same data, four ways: the raincloud

w07-groups-raincloud.R · after Heiss (2026)

  • Optional coda, after the takeaway on the previous slide: shape, sites and interval on one axis. If the block is running long, cut this slide and move straight to “From δ to F”.
  • Ask what this shows that panel 3 didn’t. Northern England’s cloud has two bumps: two kinds of site? From δ to F

Week 6: two groups, one number. δ = difference between the means.

Four groups: six differences. Testing each is the problem we just met.

One number instead:

\[F = \frac{\text{how far the group means spread}}{\text{how far sites spread within their group}}\]

  • δ was “how far apart are the means?”, judged against the null world’s spread. F asks the same of four means at once: how spread out are they, compared with the noise inside each group?
  • The bottom line is the same yardstick δ used. In Week 6 we judged δ against the spread of the null world (the SE), which came from the scatter of the boreholes.
  • With exactly two groups, F = t²: same test (Exercise 2’s extension has students check this).

“Relative to within”

w07-f-ratio.R

  • Left is our data, the same sites as the last four slides. Right: the same four means, each site moved twice as far from its region’s mean.
  • Ask which panel you would be willing to call a real regional difference, before reading the F values.
  • Doubling the scatter divides F by four: the spread enters squared.
  • Both F values come from aov() on the panel’s own points.
  • Is 14.2 big? Compared to what? The next slide builds the comparison.

The null world for F

  • Type this, don’t paste it. It is Week 6’s shuffle, line for line, with F where δ was. Students have the data; race them.

  • Anticipated code:

    wind <- read.csv("data/wind_output.csv")
f_of <- function(region) {
  summary(aov(wind$capacity_pct ~ region))[[1]][["F value"]][1]
}
f_obs <- f_of(wind$region)
f_obs

f_of(sample(wind$region))

set.seed(7)
null_f <- replicate(1000, f_of(sample(wind$region)))

ggplot(data.frame(null_f), aes(x = null_f)) +
  geom_histogram(bins = 30) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = f_obs, colour = "red")

mean(null_f >= f_obs)

  • Numbers from a real run: F = 14.21. Shuffled F values huddle near 1; the largest of 1000 is 8.8, and the 95th percentile 2.8. None reaches 14.2, so the simulated p is below 1/1000.

  • One tail only: F can’t be negative, and only a big F is surprising.

  • The shortcut: summary(aov(capacity_pct ~ region, data = wind)) gives F(3, 96) = 14.21, p = 9.8 × 10⁻⁸. Say it, don’t type it: that line is Exercise 2.

  • “This is an ANOVA”, just as last week’s shuffle was a t-test.

Corrections for multiple comparisons

Method How When
Tukey’s HSD All pairwise, designed for ANOVA After a significant ANOVA
Bonferroni Divide α by number of tests Simple, conservative, any context

Both reduce the false positive rate by being stricter about what counts as significant.

  • Don’t go deep into the math. The key idea: when you test multiple things, you raise the bar for each individual test.

“Significant” ≠ “important”

A p-value tells you whether an effect is detectable.

It does not tell you whether it’s big enough to matter.

  • [12:15–12:35] Effect sizes — the statistical formalization of “Is that a big number?”

The HolmesCo temperature problem

⚒️ HolmesCo

Geological Consultants Since 2019

“No Anomaly Too Small”™

Two boreholes, 1000 readings each.

Mean difference: 0.02 °C. p-value: 0.001.

“Highly significant temperature anomaly detected!”

Is 0.02 °C meaningful for any practical purpose?

  • With enough data, trivially small differences become “significant.” The p-value says “detectable,” not “important.”

Cohen’s d

A standardized measure of how far apart two groups are:

\[d = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{s_\text{pooled}}\]

d Interpretation
0.2 Small
0.5 Medium
0.8 Large
  • It answers “how many standard deviations apart are the groups?”

What those numbers look like

w07-cohens-d.R

  • The point is the right-hand panel. “Large” still leaves two groups overlapping across most of their range — so a significant result with d = 0.8 does not mean you can tell an individual’s group from their measurement.
  • The shared percentage is integrated from the curves, not asserted.

For ANOVA: η² (eta-squared)

Proportion of variance explained by the grouping variable.

η² Interpretation
0.01 Small
0.06 Medium
0.14 Large

Always report both the p-value and an effect size.

A policy-maker needs to know “how big?” — not just “is it real?”

Wrap-up

Key points

  1. Multiple tests inflate false positives — correct for them
  2. ANOVA compares multiple groups in one test; TukeyHSD finds which differ
  3. Significance ≠ importance — always report effect sizes
  4. “Is that a big number?” now has a formal answer
