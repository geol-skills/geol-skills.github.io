Who is your reader?

A policy-maker: busy, not a specialist, needs to make a decision.

What they need:

  • A clear bottom line
  • Evidence they can trust
  • Honest acknowledgement of uncertainty
  • [11:10–11:30] Writing for non-specialists. This is the communication strand coming to a head.

Structure of a policy briefing

Section Purpose
Summary The answer — first. Reader could stop here.
Evidence Figures, data, analysis
Caveats What’s uncertain, what depends on assumptions
Conclusions Recommendation, with caveats attached

Compare to an academic paper: background first, answer last.

A briefing is the opposite — answer first, evidence after.

  • Key principle: “Your reader should be able to stop after the summary and still know the main message.”
  • The next slide is a real briefing in exactly this shape. Open it live — preview-links: auto means the link opens in-frame.

A real parliamentary briefing

Governance of solar radiation modification research — POSTnote 773, Parliamentary Office of Science and Technology, 13 July 2026.

https://post.parliament.uk/research-briefings/post-pn-0773/ · DOI 10.58248/PN773

POST’s sections Our structure
Overview and key points Summary — the answer, first
How the research is conducted; funding figures Evidence
Geopolitics; ethics and legislation Caveats
What has been tried already Conclusions
  • The point of this slide is that the four-part shape (summary → evidence → caveats → conclusions) is general.
  • POSTnotes are peer reviewed and list their contributors at the end of the briefing. That is what “evidence they can trust” looks like in practice.

The summary test

If your reader stops after the first paragraph, do they know:

  1. What you investigated?
  2. What you found?
  3. What you recommend?
  • This is the standard for a good summary. If any of the three are missing, the summary needs work.
  • Keep the three questions live for the rest of the session. The two exemplar reports are built so that this test separates them: the faithful summary answers all three, the traitor’s answers only the middle one — loudly. Students who apply the test catch it; students who read for tone do not.

  • To avoid statistical mistakes or sleights of hand, we must recognize them.
  • One way to recognize them is to make them - deliberately!

Same data – two conclusions

Two reports present the same data in different ways.

Both are technically accurate.

One is honest. The other is not.

As a desk, annotate: - What does each report do well? - Where is it dishonest?

Links: Pro-AI | Anti-AI

  • [11:45–12:10] This is the exemplar reveal. Use the faithful and traitor AI environmental impact reports.
  • Take your time. Let students react. The moment they realize the traitor report “feels” more convincing is pedagogically powerful.

Faithful report

Traitor report

Let’s look at the techniques.

  • Show both reports (or excerpts) side by side. Students read briefly, then you walk through the techniques.

Technique 1: Cherry-picking

Choose the number that tells your story.

Example: energy per AI query — 0.3 Wh (Epoch AI’s estimate for a typical query) or 18.9 Wh (one lab’s estimate for a newer model). Same question, 63× difference, depending on which you cite.

  • The traitor report cites 18.9 Wh as the cost of “a single query”, with no hint that it is one of the highest published estimates, and sets it beside a 0.3 Wh Google search. The faithful report gives the range: 0.3–3 Wh for typical use, up to 20 Wh for reasoning models.

Technique 2: Invalid chart type

A pie chart of things that don’t form a whole.

w02-double-counted-pie.R

Or a bar chart where the y-axis doesn’t start at zero.

w02-truncated-axis.R

The chart is “technically correct” but visually lies.

  • Both of these are the Week 2 “spot the lie” charts, unchanged. Say so: the point of a callback is that they diagnosed these themselves two weeks ago and are now being asked to imagine drawing one on purpose.

Technique 3: Cross-scale conflation

Compare a small thing to a big thing without adjusting the scale.

Example: “By 2030, AI alone could be consuming more electricity than every home, factory, office, and railway in Britain combined.”

Both numbers are real: 347 TWh for AI, 319 TWh for the UK. But one is the whole world, projected for 2030; the other is one country, measured in 2024.

  • From the traitor report’s second figure: two bars of nearly equal height, with a dashed line to show AI “overtaking” the UK.
  • Ask: compared to what? Global AI against global electricity demand (about 35 000 TWh by 2030) is about 1 %.

Technique 4: Omission

What you don’t show matters as much as what you do.

Example: the traitor report never asks what an AI query replaces. A query that saves one minute of laptop time (0.45 Wh) has more than paid for its own 0.3 Wh. With no benefit side, every cost looks like pure loss.

  • Don’t reveal every technique in the teaching notes: save some for the application session reveal, when students have had a chance to spot them during peer review.
  • Keep biomass out of this example: the next exercise asks students to find the biomass omission themselves.

The lesson

The most dangerous misinformation is technically sourced.

You can’t just fact-check the numbers — you have to audit the choices.

  • This connects everything: Week 1 (falsifiability), Week 2 (“Compared to what?”), Week 3 (“What assumptions?”).
  • The skills for spotting deception are the same skills for avoiding it accidentally.

Principles (revisited)

  1. Every figure needs a “so what” — what should the reader take from it?
  2. Don’t show everything. Show the thing that matters.
  3. Captions tell the story, not just the axes.
  4. Label directly rather than relying on legends.
  • [12:20–12:40] Practical guidance for the figures going into the briefing. Show before/after examples.

w04-figure-before.R · w04-figure-after.R

  • Spend the time on the first row. Ranking the fuels by their mean over the whole period rather than by the latest year is not a cosmetic choice. Rank on 2025 and coal drops out — 0 TWh, sixth place — and the headline becomes “wind has nearly caught gas”, a 7 TWh gap.
  • Rank on the mean and coal is back in second place, and you can see the thing that actually happened: coal and wind swapped, around 2015. In 2024, the last year DUKES records any coal (2.0 TWh), wind out-generated it roughly forty times over.
  • So: the honest chart is the one that picked its four fuels by a rule chosen before looking at which story it produced. Both rules are defensible. Only one of them was written down first.
  • This is the Week 2 refrain arriving in their own work: compared to what? And the Week 4 refrain: audit the choices, not just the numbers.
  • Also note the importance of colour: this uses the Durham palette. My first draft swapped the colours of coal and gas - semantic linkage made this hard to read!

The briefing assignment

📋🎓 Assignment and traitors

  • Wrap-up

Your assignment

Write a ~2-page policy briefing answering:

“Does UK biomass power help reach net zero, and should we keep paying for it?”

Due: committed to your GitHub repo by the end of the application session.

Structure: summary → evidence (with figures) → caveats → conclusions.

  • [12:40–12:50] Hand out the assignment details. This is formative — it doesn’t count toward the final grade, but it’s essential practice.

Peer review

You will each be assigned two classmates’ briefings.

One Issue per briefing — clarity, evidence, uncertainty, verdict.

The review form opens with two drop-downs a script reads:

Verdict   FAITHFUL / SUSPECT
Argues    KEEP-OR-EXPAND / KEEP-ONLY-WITH-CCS / PHASE-OUT
  • Labour the two drop-downs. They are how the reveal gets built. Repos accepted before 2026-10-05 have no form: those reviewers copy the block from the Peer review template page, and a mangled answer there drops that review out of the class data.
  • Verdict and Argues are separate on purpose — flag that now, come back to it in the reveal.

Two questions, not one

Do you believe it? — and, separately — what is it arguing?

A briefing can reach the conclusion you already hold and still be a traitor’s.

  • This is the whole reason Argues exists as a field. Students reliably conflate “I agree with the conclusion” with “this is honest”, and the reveal chart is built to show them doing it.

The traitors

Some of you will write deliberately misleading briefings.

You are not choosing. If you are a traitor, you have already been told — privately, and only you were told.

Everyone else: read carefully. Not everything is what it seems — including the things you agree with.

  • Traitors are allocated, not volunteers, and notified privately before the session. Do not state the traitor rate: not knowing how many there are is part of the exercise.
  • Nobody will be asked to identify themselves, in this session or the next. Say so — it lowers the stakes and improves the writing.
  • Keep this light. The traitors are doing the class a favour.
