Who is your reader?

11:10

Policy-makers are busy non-specialists who need to make a decision they can stand by.

What they need:

  • A clear bottom line
  • Evidence that bears the weight of the conclusion
  • Clarity on what’s uncertain
  • [11:10–11:20] Writing for non-specialists. This is the communication strand coming to a head.
  • Uncertainty: Which “fact”’s gonna turn around and bite them on the ass?

Structure of a policy briefing

11:15
Section Purpose
Summary The answer. Reader could stop here.
Evidence Figures, data, analysis
Caveats Uncertainty, assumptions: what’s a journalist going to hurl at me?
Conclusions Considered recommendation

An academic paper / dissertation / PPDAC cycle goes the other way round: progressively build up to an answer.

  • Key principle: “Your reader should be able to stop after the summary and still know the main message.”
  • Evidence: How big is the problem being solved? Why is this the solution?
  • Caveats: Prepare for scrutiny by the opposition / Lords / public / Today
  • “Considered” recommendation → How strongly should the policy-maker argue?

Inspect the text

11:20

Handout: §Solar biomass, from David MacKay, Sustainable Energy – without the hot air (2008), pp. 42–44.

Use three different colours to highlight:

  1. Claim: what MacKay wants the reader to take away
  2. Evidence: what the claim is based on
  3. Caveat: what limits the claim (e.g. assumptions)

Text: withouthotair.com, CC BY-NC-SA 2.0 UK.

  • About the book: Mackay was sick of greenwash: vague but unsubstantiated claims of how we could live sustainably. His one desideratum: Numbers that add up! The book asks the question: What would a sustainably-powered UK look like?

  • Nine months after it came out he was Chief Scientific Adviser at DECC. The numbers, well communicated, drove policy.

  • Converts the numbers to kWh per person and works through one source/sink of energy at a time. Here we’ve reached biomass.

  • NOTE: The numbers are from 2008. The approach is timeless. “Do the numbers add up?”. “Is that a big number? Compared to what?”

  • [11:20–11:26] Hand out pp. 42–44, and highlighters. Students read alone, then highlight on paper.

  • Anticipated highlights:

    • Claim: biofuels “can’t add up”, in Britain at least, and not as a replacement for all transport fuels; at most 24 kWh/d per person, and he thinks that’s an over-estimate.
    • Evidence: sunlight 100 W/m²; best European crops 0.5 W/m²; 75 % of the country = 3000 m² per person; 0.5 × 3000 → 36 kWh/d per person; lose at least a third in processing → 24.
    • Caveats: 75 % of the country is “outrageously generous”; the figure ignores growing, harvesting and processing; food competes for the land; farming inputs can cancel the delivered energy; no one predicts better than 1 W/m², even with genetic engineering; Fig. 6.11’s caption: wood manages only 0.095–0.254 W/m².

Discuss with your desk:

11:26
  1. What is MacKay’s main claim?
  2. What evidence supports it?
  3. What does it assume or omit?
  4. What real-world decisions might be influenced by this perspective? (How?)

Where on the page was the claim? The caveats?

  • [11:26–11:32] Easy to hard: the first three are in the text; the fourth is an inference. MacKay doesn’t name a decision-maker.
  • Reflection: the answer is the last paragraph, after a page and a half of arithmetic. Caveats are everywhere: in the figure caption, mid-sentence asides, a closing aside about food. That is the right order for a textbook, which builds the case for a reader with time. A briefing reverses it (Concept block 1).
  • Q4 answers vary: a transport minister weighing biofuel mandates is the reader MacKay’s conclusion fits best. Hold that thought.

Briefing the minister

11:32

The UK imports 9.3 million tonnes of pellets annually, equivalent to 1.7 kWh/d per person of fuel.

The Secretary of State for Energy Security and Net Zero is looking for ways to reduce our reliance on imported pellets to improve our energy security and cut transport emissions.

Discuss with your neighbour: what would you tell the minister about the feasibility of replacing imported wood pellets with home-grown biomass?

  • [11:32–11:35] The sum: 1.7 / 36 × 75 % ≈ 3.6 % of the UK, at best-crop yields.
  • Pellets are wood. At Fig. 6.11’s 0.095–0.254 W/m² it is about 20 000–53 000 km²: 8–22 % of the UK. The UK’s woodland is about 13 %. County Durham is about 2200 km²: 4–5 County Durhams at best, 9–24 as forest.
  • 1.7 kWh/d per person (and MacKay’s 36) is chemical energy, before conversion to electricity.
  • Source: data/pellet_imports.csv (2024 sums to 9317 kt); 17 GJ/t from data/README.md. Population 69.3 million (ONS mid-2024).

Writing a summary

11:34

If your reader stops after the first paragraph, do they know:

  1. What you investigated?
  2. What you found?
  3. What you recommend?
  • This is the standard for a good summary. If any of the three are missing, the summary needs work.
  • Keep the three questions live for the rest of the session. The two exemplar reports are built so that this test separates them: the faithful summary answers all three, the traitor’s answers only the middle one — loudly. Students who apply the test catch it; students who read for tone do not.

Write the summary

Write a 3–4 sentence summary of a briefing on the feasibility of replacing imported wood pellets with home-grown biomass

Pass your summary paragraph to your left. Read your neighbour’s:

  • Is the message clear?
  • Is ambiguity acknowledged?

At home: Rewrite the summary in your drax briefing the same way.

  • [11:35–11:45] Seven minutes writing, three sharing. Push for the number in the first sentence, with a comparator: “4 % of the UK”, “four County Durhams”. Compared to what?
  • A summary that would pass: “Growing Drax’s fuel in the UK would take at least 10 000 km², about 4 % of the country, even with the best energy crops, and two to five times that as forest. A real parliamentary briefing

11:45

Supply chains for low carbon electricity infrastructure — POSTnote 782, Parliamentary Office of Science and Technology.

https://post.parliament.uk/research-briefings/post-pn-0782/ · DOI 10.58248/PN782

Read it (5 minutes), then discuss with your neighbours:

  1. How does it map onto summary → evidence → caveats → conclusions?
  2. What is the effect of a more fluid structure?
  3. Is a standard template helpful, or overly restrictive?
  • [11:45–11:55] Students have just forced MacKay into the template. Now: how far does a real briefing bend it?
  • Students read the briefing themselves rather than being given the mapping. Open it live — preview-links: auto opens it in-frame.
  • Likely observations. The opening paragraph is the summary, and it stands alone. The body is a run of questions as headings (“What are the main supply chain risks?”), so the reader can jump to the question they have. Caveats are not a section: they are woven through (e.g. “the exact mapping of the supply chain constraints … is yet to be published”; “gaps in the analysis”). The closing section lists options, not a single recommendation — POST is neutral by remit. Contributors are listed at the end; that is “evidence they can trust” in practice.
  • Fluid structure: reads naturally and fits the topic, but caveats can be missed or buried, and the reader must work to find the uncertainty. Template: guarantees the uncertainty gets its own place and helps novices, but can force a recommendation the evidence doesn’t support, or split a caveat from the claim it qualifies.
  • Steer towards: the template is scaffolding for people learning to write briefings, and experts adapt it to the audience. Ask what the minister’s office would lose if caveats appeared only inline.

Cherry-picking

12:03

Choose the number that tells your story.

Example: energy per AI query — 0.3 Wh (Epoch AI’s estimate for a typical query) or 18.9 Wh (one lab’s estimate for a newer model). A handy 63× difference can stack a comparison in your favour!

Antidote?

  • The traitor report cites 18.9 Wh as the cost of “a single query”, with no hint that it is one of the highest published estimates, and sets it beside a 0.3 Wh Google search. The faithful report gives the range: 0.3–3 Wh for typical use, up to 20 Wh for reasoning models.
  • Ask students for the antidote: Cite a range? Acknowledge the context?

Cross-scale conflation

12:12

Compare a small thing to a big thing without adjusting the scale.

“By 2030, AI alone could be consuming more electricity than every home, factory, office, and railway in Britain combined.”

Numbers are legit (347 TWh for AI > 319 TWh for the UK)

Comparison is not (Whole world, projected for 2030 > one country, measured in 2024)

  • From the traitor report’s second figure: two bars of nearly equal height, with a dashed line to show AI “overtaking” the UK.
  • Ask: compared to what? Global AI against global electricity demand (about 35 000 TWh by 2030) is about 1 %.

Omission

12:16

What you don’t show matters as much as what you do.

Never neglect the counterfactual.

What would have happened otherwise?

An AI query costs 0.3 Wh.

Counterfactual: a task takes a minute longer

One minute of laptop time costs 0.45 Wh

One minute of human time (at 75 kWh/day) costs 52 Wh

  • 27 000 kWh/year is UK primary energy per person: DUKES 2026 gives 162.5 Mtoe for 2025 (× 11.63 MWh/toe ≈ 1890 TWh), over about 69 million people. That is about 75 kWh/day; MacKay’s Without the Hot Air puts it at 125 kWh/day once imported goods are counted.
  • 27 000 000 Wh ÷ 525 960 min/year ≈ 52 Wh, about 170 AI queries.

Before and after

Before After

Which change did the most work?

Series All six fuels The four largest by mean over 2000–2025

w04-figure-before.R · w04-figure-after.R

  • Spend the time on the first row. Ranking the fuels by their mean over the whole period rather than by the latest year is not a cosmetic choice. Rank on 2025 and coal drops out — 0 TWh, sixth place — and the headline becomes “wind has nearly caught gas”, a 7 TWh gap.
  • Rank on the mean and coal is back in second place, and you can see the thing that actually happened: coal and wind swapped, around 2015. In 2024, the last year DUKES records any coal (2.0 TWh), wind out-generated it roughly forty times over.
  • So: the honest chart is the one that picked its four fuels by a rule chosen before looking at which story it produced. Both rules are defensible. Only one of them was written down first.
  • This is the Week 2 refrain arriving in their own work: compared to what? And the Week 4 refrain: audit the choices, not just the numbers.
  • Also note the importance of colour: this uses the Durham palette. My first draft swapped the colours of coal and gas - semantic linkage made this hard to read!
