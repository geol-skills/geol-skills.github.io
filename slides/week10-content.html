The policy report

11:10
Section Length Purpose
Summary 200–300 words The finding, the evidence, the recommendation
Introduction 200–300 words The question, why it matters
Methods 250–400 words Data, approach, why these tests
Results 500–750 words Figures, tests, plain-language interpretation
Discussion ~500 words What it means, caveats, recommendation
  • [11:10–11:25] Walk through each section using the Phase 1 faithful exemplar (exemplar/ai-good), open in a separate tab: no slide shows it.
  • Methods: could someone clone the repo and redo the analysis? Results: each test with its effect size or CI, in plain language. The Week 10 review checklist asks exactly this; don’t list it twice.

Trade summaries

11:25

Swap your draft summary with someone working on a different topic.

As a reader, answer from the summary alone:

  1. What is the main finding?
  2. Do I trust it?
  3. What would I do with this information?

Then tell the author what you understood. If it isn’t what they meant, the summary needs work.

  • [11:25–11:40] Students write (if they haven’t) or refine, then swap and discuss.
  • A different topic, not just a different group: the reader knows nothing about the project, as the marker won’t, and nobody worries about sharing work with a rival on the same question.

Every line equally loud

11:43

w10-annotation-before.R · idea after Heiss (2026)

Shows the data Explains the data Facilitates comparison

  • This is Week 4’s “after” standard: axes, units, title, source, legend ordered by size. Nothing here is wrong. Ask: what is this figure about? Expect several answers — that is the problem.
  • Watch the room shuttle between legend and lines. Then ask which line is bioenergy: gas and bioenergy are two near-identical dark colours. Six series is more than a legend can carry.
  • Numbers for narration, from the script (shares of total generation, 2025): gas 31.7 %, wind 29.4 %, bioenergy 14.0 %, nuclear 12.2 %, solar 6.8 %, coal 0.0 %.

One story, labelled directly

11:47

w10-annotation-after.R · idea after Heiss (2026)

Shows the data Explains the data Facilitates comparison

  • Same data, same scales. Three decisions, and each is one you can make in your report:
    1. Label the lines directly. The legend is gone; the eye never leaves the data.
    2. Highlight the story, grey the rest. The grey lines stay: they answer “compared to what?” without competing.
    3. Annotate the point that matters. 2017 is the first year bioenergy generated more than coal (9.4 % vs 6.7 %; in 2016 coal was still just ahead, 9.1 % vs 8.9 %). The script finds the year; nobody typed it.
  • The title now states the finding: write the headline first, then shape the figure so the headline is what the reader sees (Krzywinski & Cairo, on the reading list).
  • Greying four lines is editing. Deleting gas and wind, so that bioenergy looked like the thing that replaced coal, would be advocacy: wind grew far more. That is the Week 4 traitor line.
  • The labels are placed without an add-on package: the script nudges them apart just far enough not to overlap. Students can open it.
  • For more on labels: Cara Thompson, Level up your labels (useR! 2022).

Emotion shapes perception

12:05

People weigh a risk by how it feels, rather than its probability:

  • Dreaded, unfamiliar, imposed risks loom large
  • Familiar, chosen risks are overlooked

A690 crash, November 2026: Michelle Kearney / The Northern Echo

Counts are more relatable than percentages: “about 1 journey in 1000”, not “0.1 %” or “risk doubled” (compared to what?)

  • [12:05–12:15]
  • Slovic (1987), “Perception of risk”, Science 236: 280–285: dread and unknown risk are the two factors that predict public concern.
  • Counts are Week 6’s natural frequencies: the medical test worked out of 100 000 people because counts are how readers think. “Risk doubled” with no baseline is a pitfall: doubled from a low base?

Framing shapes the message

12:10

“99 % of young Londoners do not commit serious youth violence”

Or in other words: 1 % of young Londoners do commit serious violence.

Or: Of London’s 1 million young people, 10 000 commit serious youth violence

That one didn’t make the poster.

What framings are available for the key numbers in your report? What is the emotional impact of each?

  • The advert ran on the London Underground. Spiegelhalter uses it to open his discussion of framing (The Art of Statistics, ch. 1): “99 % do not” reassures, “1 % do” alarms, and “10 000 young people” alarms most. All three are true.
  • Check before use: the chapter and page, and the 10 000 (it assumes about a million Londoners in the age range). Not verified when this slide was written.
  • Tie to their reports: “98 % of boreholes met the standard” and “1 in 50 failed” are the same result. An honest report picks the frame its reader needs, and where it matters gives both.

The process

12:15

Before the application session:

  1. Commit and push a complete draft.
  2. Tag it for-review and push the tag. That version is the one reviewed.
    GitHub Desktop: History → right-click the commit → Create Tag…, then Push.

Until you’ve pushed, it’s not left your computer!

You will need to tag final to submit. Practise on for-review!

NB: a final tag can’t be undone.

During the application sesson: review a report on a different topic, then debrief and revise.

  • [12:15–12:27] Review assignments arrive at the start of the application session, as an Issue on each student’s report repo.
  • Reviewers read the for-review tag (on GitHub, the branch menu → Tags), so later pushes don’t change what they review. A student who tags nothing is reviewed at whatever was pushed last.
  • for-review is unprotected, so a student who tags the wrong commit can delete the tag and make it again; final allows no second try.
  • [12:27–12:52] Students decide. Spare time, provisional until the Week 8 read-through: Week 8 overspill if we are behind, otherwise whatever the students ask for (the Q&A slide that follows is one way in).
