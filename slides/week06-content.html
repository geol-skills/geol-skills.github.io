Is the difference real?

Boreholes in the Stainmore Formation run hotter, on average, than boreholes in the Whin Sill.

“Is this difference larger than we’d expect by chance?”

The Whin Sill at Holwick Scars, Teesdale.
Gordon Hatton / Geograph, CC BY-SA 2.0

  • [11:10–11:42] The null world by simulation, then the t-test as its shortcut, each with a live demo.
  • Don’t say “t-test” yet. The whole block builds one idea, the null world, and only names the formula once students own the idea. The reveal is “This is a t-test”, after the null-world demo.

Five steps, every test

  1. Measure δ — the one number you care about. Here: Stainmore’s mean temperature minus Whin Sill’s.
  2. Build a null world — a world where δ really is zero. Simulate it: shuffle the formation labels, recompute δ, repeat.
  3. Drop your δ into it. Does it look at home?
  4. Count how often the null world makes a δ at least as extreme. That fraction is the p-value.
  5. Decide, against a standard you set before you looked.
  • Steps 2–4 are the heart of it. Today we do them by brute force, by shuffling; the named tests come later and only replace those steps with a formula.
  • The idea that every test is this one test is Allen Downey’s (“There is only one test”); the five-step framing and the interactive version are Andrew Heiss’s Null worlds, https://nullworlds.andrewheiss.com/. Point students there — the sliders make the intuition stick.
  • The whole method rests on one question: how surprising would this be if nothing were going on? Plant a flag here. In block 3 we will meet its partner — how plausible was this before we tested? — and the gap between the two is the base-rate fallacy. A null world only ever answers the first question.

A world where formation doesn’t matter

w06-null-world.R · After Heiss (2026), Null worlds

  • Left is step 1: the data as sampled. δ = 8.4 °C.
  • Right is step 2: keep every temperature exactly as measured, but deal the formation labels out again at random, 1000 times. In every one of those worlds formation cannot matter — the labels are noise — so the spread of δ is what chance alone produces with these boreholes.
  • Step 3: the dashed lines are our real δ, both signs, because the question is “this extreme”, not “this large”.
  • Step 4: count the red. 26 of 1000, so p = 0.026.
  • Hold on to this picture: after the reveal, “What ‘how often’ means” shows a smooth curve with shaded tails. That curve is the formula’s version of this histogram.

Build the null world

  • Type this, don’t paste it. Students have the same data; invite them to beat you to the p-value.

  • Anticipated code:

    borehole <- read.csv("data/borehole_temp.csv")
temp <- borehole$temperature_c

delta <- mean(temp[borehole$formation == "Stainmore"]) -
  mean(temp[borehole$formation == "Whin Sill"])
delta

shuffled <- sample(borehole$formation)
mean(temp[shuffled == "Stainmore"]) - mean(temp[shuffled == "Whin Sill"])

set.seed(6)
null_deltas <- replicate(1000, {
  shuffled <- sample(borehole$formation)
  mean(temp[shuffled == "Stainmore"]) - mean(temp[shuffled == "Whin Sill"])
})

ggplot(data.frame(null_deltas), aes(x = null_deltas)) +
  geom_histogram(bins = 30) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = c(-delta, delta), colour = "red")

mean(abs(null_deltas) >= abs(delta))

sd(null_deltas)

  • Numbers from a real run: δ = 8.417; p = 0.026 (26 of 1000 shuffles). The null deltas run from about −11.4 to +11.1.

  • Step 3 is unseeded on purpose: run it two or three times so the room sees each shuffle give a different δ, near zero. Then set.seed(6) immediately before replicate() — that is what the figure on the previous slide used, so the p-value matches it exactly. Put the seed anywhere else and the third decimal moves; that wobble is itself worth a sentence: a simulated p-value is an estimate, and 1000 shuffles is plenty to tell 0.03 from 0.3.

  • abs() twice makes it two-sided: “this extreme”, either way.

  • Step 7 gives about 3.9 °C (the exact value depends on the seed). Don’t explain it yet: it is the hook for the next slide.

SD or SE?

w06-sd-or-se.R

SD: spread of the boreholes. SE: spread of δ between samples.

  • [2 min] Students met SD in Weeks 1 and 2; SE is new. Every later week leans on it (Week 7’s ± 1.96 SE, Week 8’s slope SE).
  • Left column: “more points keep the same scatter”: SD 20 and 9 °C in both rows. Right column: “but with more points, a shuffle is less likely to put the means far apart by chance”, so the null world narrows from about 7.5 °C to 3.9 °C.
  • The SD describes the data; the SE describes a statistic (here δ) across the samples we might have drawn. The null-world histogram is exactly that picture: the bottom right is the null world the room just built, and its SE is the sd(null_deltas) they just saw.
  • Formula, if asked: SE of δ = √(SD₁²/n₁ + SD₂²/n₂), which gives 3.75 °C at n = 35 per formation, close to the shuffle’s 3.94 °C. The n under the root is why more data narrows the SE while leaving the SD where it is.
  • The 8-per-formation subset is real boreholes, drawn at random. Numbers: sd() gives 20.26 °C (Stainmore) and 9.10 °C (Whin Sill).

What would the null world be?

For each claim: what would you shuffle, simulate, or hold fixed?

  1. The same 15 boreholes, logged in 2005 and again in 2025, read warmer now.
  2. HolmesCo struck water in 8 of its 10 boreholes, beating the regional rate of 50 %.
  3. Downstream wells have more sulphate than upstream ones. The wells were sampled in upstream–downstream pairs, one pair per site.
  • [3 min, within 11:10–11:42] Desks, one minute per claim. Ask for the null world, not a test name. Each claim needs a different step 2.
  • 1: paired. Within each borehole, swap the two readings at random (equivalently, flip the sign of each 2025 − 2005 difference). Shuffling across boreholes would mix in the differences between sites, which is not what the claim is about. Aside: even a clear result answers “chance?”, not “why?” (newer probes? Week 5’s confounders).
  • 2: simulate from a model. There are no labels to shuffle. Flip a fair coin 10 times, count the heads, repeat 1000 times; how often do we get 8 or more?
  • 3: constrained shuffle. A free shuffle breaks the pairing: sites differ in geology, so sulphate varies more between sites than within them. Swap the labels only within each site. Good place to plant the word “assumption” for block 2: every null world assumes something about which values could have traded places.
  • The point to drill: the same five steps every time, but step 2 is where the thinking is.

This is a t-test

A t-test asks the same question with a formula, instead of a shuffle:

  1. Assume no difference: the null world (the null hypothesis)
  2. Calculate how far apart the means are, relative to the variability (→ t-statistic)
  3. Ask: if there were really no difference, how often would we see a t this extreme? (→ p-value)
  • The reveal. Map each line back to the five steps: line 1 is step 2, lines 2–3 are steps 3–4.
  • The payoff line: “Everything t.test() does is a shortcut for what we just did by hand.” Shuffling needs no normality assumption — but it still compares means, and Stainmore’s long tail drags its mean. Block 2 comes back to that, and it is not the test’s fault.
  • The formula buys speed and a smooth curve; it costs assumptions about the shape of the data. Next demo: does it agree with our 0.026?

Every test rests on assumptions

For the t-test:

  1. Independence — observations don’t influence each other
  2. Normality — data within each group are roughly normal
  3. Equal variance — groups have similar spread (Student’s t; Welch’s t relaxes this)
  • [11:50–12:05] Assumptions, how to check, what to do when violated. Keep “How to check” to a quick pass: name Levene’s test but don’t demo it. The skew figure is where the time should go.

How to check

Assumption Check
Normality Histogram, QQ plot, shapiro.test()
Equal variance Side-by-side points or violins, Levene’s test
Independence Think about the study design

When assumptions are violated

A skewed variable can produce misleading p-values.

w06-skew-and-logs.R

  • This is the dataset from the live demo. The test they just ran is the one that breaks: p = 0.030 raw, p = 0.22 logged. Both p-values are computed by the script.
  • Nothing was removed and no outlier was excluded. The only change is the scale the test was run on.
  • Someone may ask: the shuffle test assumed nothing about normality and still gave 0.03 — so was the t-test really broken? Shuffle the log temperatures and you get p ≈ 0.23, close to the logged t-test’s 0.22. Both machines agree on whichever δ you hand them; the transform changes the question (typical ratio, not mean difference in °C), and with a tail like Stainmore’s the mean is the wrong question.

Warm-up: Monty Hall

  • [12:15–12:40] Take your time. This is the intellectual centrepiece of the module. Let the numbers sink in.

Some of you simulated the Monty Hall problem in Python last year.

Switching wins 2/3 of the time. But your gut says 50/50.

Why? Because your gut ignores the information the host gave you when they opened a door.

The distinction between P(win) and P(win | what you now know) is also the crux of the base rate fallacy.

  • Brief connection. Don’t re-derive Monty Hall. The point: conditional probability is unintuitive, and this has serious consequences.

The medical test

A disease affects 1 in 1000 people.

A test is 99 % accurate (99 % sensitivity, 99 % specificity).

You test positive.

What’s the probability you have the disease?

The arithmetic

Out of 100 000 people:

Has disease No disease Total
Test positive 99 999 1098
Test negative 1 98 901 98 902
Total 100 99 900 100 000

P(disease | positive) = 99 / 1098 ≈ 9 %

Not 99 %. Nine percent.

  • Six clicks. The table opens on the base rate alone: 100 with the disease, 99 900 without.
  • Click 1 sweeps that row down to open the test rows. Click 2: the 99 / 1 split among the 100 who have it. Click 3: the 999 / 98 901 split among the 99 900 who don’t. Click 4: the positive and negative totals. Click 5: the 9 %. Click 6: “Nine percent.”
  • The margins carry the argument: 100 against 99 900, then 99 against
  • Let this land. Pause. “Why is it so low?”

Why is it so low?

Because the disease is rare.

The base rate matters enormously.

w06-base-rate.R

Even a very accurate test produces many false positives when the condition is uncommon.

  • The 9 % from the previous slide is the marked point. Same test, same 99 % accuracy, everywhere on this curve — only the population changes.
  • Ask where they would have to move along the axis to get the 99 % they guessed in the poll. It is a condition affecting half the room.

Assay positive
Assay negative

  • Desks, 3–4 minutes. They have just watched you fill in the medical table, so give no more help than “same layout as before”.
  • Common first guess: 51 (95 % of 54). Ask them to start from the bottom row: how many samples have gold at all?

The reality

Gold present No gold Total
Assay positive ~1 ~50 ~51
Assay negative ~0 ~949 ~949
Total 1 999 1000

Expected: about 51 hits, 1 of them real. HolmesCo found 54.

Is 54 different from 51? Is it different from 50 hits and no gold?

  • True positives: 1 × 0.95 ≈ 1. False positives: 999 × 0.05 ≈ 50. P(gold | positive) ≈ 1 / 51 ≈ 2 %.
  • Let desks argue about 54 vs 51. By chance alone the false-positive count varies by about ±7 (binomial SD, √(999 × 0.05 × 0.95) ≈ 6.9), so 54 is unremarkable: it is what the assay produces if there is almost no gold at all. The press release is nonsense.
  • This is the Week 6 equivalent of the traitor reveal.

Connection to p-values

A p-value tells you: P(data | H₀)

How surprising is this data if the null is true?

What you actually want: P(H₀ | data)

How likely is the null to be true given this data?

These are not the same thing. The difference depends on the base rate.

  • Call back to the null world: every p-value today answered “how surprising would this be if nothing were going on?” The refrain on the next slide is the question the null world cannot answer.
  • Key sentence: “If you’re testing an implausible hypothesis, a significant p-value is probably a false positive. If you’re testing something well-supported, a significant result is more convincing.”

“How plausible was this before we tested?”

  • This is the Bayesian insight without the formula. The habit of asking about prior plausibility is what matters.
  • This also sets up Week 7: if you run 20 tests, the base rate for any individual hypothesis being true is low → false positives accumulate.

Wrap-up

Key points

  1. A t-test compares two groups — but check the assumptions first
  2. A p-value is P(data | H₀), not P(H₀ | data)
  3. The base rate determines whether a “significant” result is real
  4. Always ask: “How plausible was this before I tested?”
