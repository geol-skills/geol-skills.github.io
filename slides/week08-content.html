Why did surgeons think it worked?

11:10

Patients felt better after surgery. Three stories fit that:

  1. The operation fixed the problem
  2. The body healed on its own
  3. Being cared for made them feel better (placebo)

Pooling 100 surgical trials: about two-thirds of the improvement was stories 2 and 3.

  • Squares: confounding, placebo, effect size.
  • Recovery and ritual always travel with the operation, so an uncontrolled before/after comparison credits the scalpel with all three. That is confounding in its purest form.
  • “Is that a big number?” One-third of the benefit is still a benefit; the question is whether it justifies the risk and cost.
  • Ask: which square did you put this under? Several answers are right.

How do you separate them?

11:11
Compare Isolates
Surgery vs sham surgery the operation itself
Sham surgery vs no treatment the placebo
No treatment, before vs after natural recovery

One 2002 trial splashed water into a dish so the sham sounded right.

  • Squares: control group, randomization, blinding, “Compared to what?”.
  • Each row is a control group answering a different question. Most of the benefit in the sham arm turned out to be natural recovery, not placebo.
  • Moseley’s knee trial (165 patients): the team matched even sounds a patient under general anaesthetic might register. Blinding taken seriously.
  • A 2020 review: only about 1 % of trials for chronic musculoskeletal pain compared surgery with doing nothing at all.

Why were surgeons so sure?

11:13
  • Patients who come back for check-ups are mostly the ones who got better
  • Those who come back don’t want to disappoint their surgeon
  • Surgeons’ predictions of who will improve: no better than a coin toss
  • Squares: survivorship bias, reporting bias, confirmation bias.
  • The surgeon’s evidence is their own follow-up clinic. The failures have gone elsewhere; the successes are polite. Years of experience build confidence on a biased sample.
  • “Confidently be unconfident” (Blazeby) is a good line to leave on the screen verbally.

The square most people missed

11:15

Scans find tears and “abnormalities” in knees, backs and shoulders.

The same abnormalities are common in people with no pain at all.

How plausible was this before we tested?

  • Square: base rate neglect (Week 6).
  • We want P(abnormality causes pain | abnormality seen). If most pain-free people of the same age have it too, finding it in a patient tells you very little. Same structure as HolmesCo’s gold assay.
  • Also worth a minute: the paid-per-operation vs salaried contrast (UK shoulder operations fell by about four-fifths after the trials; US numbers did not). Is that a fair comparison? Countries differ in far more than how surgeons are paid: confounding again.

In geology nobody says the word “placebo”. Would you spot these?

One tail or two?

11:18

w08-tails.R

  • [11:18–11:26] A refresher on Week 6 as much as a new idea. The null world: shuffle the formation labels, recompute δ, 1000 times; the p-value is the share of shuffles as extreme as ours.
  • “As extreme” in which direction? Two-tailed: either (22 of 1000, p = 0.022). One-tailed, “is Stainmore warmer?”: only the upper tail (13 of 1000, p = 0.013). Same null world, about half the p-value.
  • t.test() does the same: two-sided p = 0.030; alternative = "greater" gives 0.015, exactly half.

HolmesCo’s latest results

11:21

⚒️ HolmesCo

Geological Consultants Since 2019

“Ironing Out Your Problems”™

Iron Remediation Results

As advised, we sampled upstream and downstream of every treated site, and tested a competitor’s product too.

  • FerroGone: significant difference, p = 0.03
  • ClearFlow: no significant difference, p = 0.07

FerroGone: the only proven choice.

  • Present it straight: “HolmesCo took our advice! Upstream and downstream pairs, like Week 6, and a competitor for comparison.” Let the room enjoy p = 0.03 before step 4.

  • Anticipated code:

    head(iron)

fg <- iron[iron$product == "FerroGone", ]
t.test(fg$downstream_mg_l, fg$upstream_mg_l, paired = TRUE)

cf <- iron[iron$product == "ClearFlow", ]
t.test(cf$downstream_mg_l, cf$upstream_mg_l, paired = TRUE)

t.test(fg$downstream_mg_l, fg$upstream_mg_l, paired = TRUE,
       alternative = "less")
t.test(cf$downstream_mg_l, cf$upstream_mg_l, paired = TRUE,
       alternative = "less")

  • Numbers (10 sites each, simulated): FerroGone mean difference +0.75 mg/L, p = 0.032. ClearFlow −0.47 mg/L, p = 0.069.

  • Twist 1: FerroGone’s “significant difference” is iron going up. Don’t buy it. HolmesCo asked “any difference?” when the only question that matters is “does it lower iron?”: one-tailed, alternative = "less" (downstream − upstream < 0). FerroGone, one-tailed: p = 0.98.

  • Twist 2: ClearFlow, one-tailed: p = 0.034. Ten sites was too few for a two-tailed test: if the true fall is 0.47 mg/L, ten sites detect it two-tailed less than half the time (power 0.45); about 21 would give 80 %.

  • Yes, we picked the tail after seeing the data. That is the next slide’s warning: the question was one-tailed from the start, and should have been written down before anyone sampled.

  • paired = TRUE is a one-sample t-test on the differences, the constrained shuffle from Week 6’s “What would the null world be?”

Mine water: only better counts

11:23

Pool stained with iron, Blast Beach, Seaham: Andrew Curtis / Geograph, CC BY-SA 2.0

Orange water from old coal workings: a treatment scheme should cut its iron.

Has iron fallen? If it don’t cut iron it don’t work: if it increases iron I’m definitely not paying for it!

The other tail isn’t interesting: one-tailed.

Define your queston before you ask it.

Switching to one-tailed after a two-tailed fails is a second test – and a second chance for a false positive.

  • In R: t.test(iron ~ period, data = water, alternative = "less"). The alternative is about the first group minus the second, in alphabetical order: “after” − “before”, so “less” means iron fell. Check which way round R took it.
  • A one-tailed test can’t see an effect the other way. If an increase would matter (would we close the scheme, or sue?), test both tails.
  • The key point leads into the next section: doubling the chances of a false positive is the multiple comparisons problem in miniature.

The problem

11:26

Three groups (e.g., wind farms in three regions).

You could run three pairwise t-tests: A vs B, A vs C, B vs C.

Each test, on its own, has a 5 % chance of a false positive.

Run 20 tests where nothing is going on. How often do you “find” something?

  • [11:26–11:43] Multiple comparisons. Take guesses before the next slide; most of the room will say 5 % or “once”.

Chance in action

11:30

  • Type this, don’t paste it. Same move as Week 6’s null world: build a world where nothing is going on, then watch what the test does there.

  • Anticipated code:

    t.test(rnorm(25), rnorm(25))$p.value

survey <- replicate(20, t.test(rnorm(25), rnorm(25))$p.value)
sum(survey < 0.05)

set.seed(7)
hits <- replicate(1000, {
  survey <- replicate(20, t.test(rnorm(25), rnorm(25))$p.value)
  sum(survey < 0.05)
})
mean(hits >= 1)
table(hits)

  • Run steps 1 and 2 unseeded, three or four times each: the room sees p bounce around, and some surveys turn up one or two “discoveries”.

  • Step 3, from a real run with set.seed(7) just before replicate(): 67 % of surveys find at least one; 76 of 1000 (about 1 in 13) find three or more. Theory says 64 %; the difference is simulation noise.

  • Then the shortcut, exactly as the t-test was a shortcut for the shuffle: 1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ 0.64. Put the formula on the board; it reappears in Exercise 1.

HolmesCo’s mineral survey

11:35

⚒️ HolmesCo

Geological Consultants Since 2019

“Leave No Stone Untested”™

Press Release — Multi-Element Anomaly Discovered!

“We tested soil samples from 20 sites for enrichment in 20 elements. We found statistically significant enrichment (p < 0.05) for three elements at one site!”

If there’s truly nothing there, how many significant results would you expect by chance?

  • Their survey is 20 of our simulated surveys, one per site. Students can reason from the last slide: 20 expected false positives, about one per site.

The answer

11:39

20 × 20 = 400 tests at α = 0.05

Expected false positives: 20, about one per site.

With nothing there at all, some site scores three or more four times in five.

The “discovery” is what noise looks like.

  • Each site’s hits are binomial(20, 0.05): one site reaches three with probability 0.075; across 20 sites, 1 − 0.925²⁰ ≈ 0.79.
  • In the simulation, about 1 survey in 13 found three or more by itself. With 20 sites, one of them doing so is the expected outcome.
  • Ask what the press release leaves out: the total number of significant results across all 400 tests, and which site HolmesCo would have predicted before looking. They picked the site after seeing which came out best.

Same data, four ways: the bar

w08-groups-bar.R

From δ to F

11:55

Week 6: two groups, one number. δ = difference between the means.

Four groups: six differences. Testing each is the problem we just met.

One number instead:

\[F = \frac{\text{how far the group means spread}}{\text{how far sites spread within their group}}\]

  • δ was “how far apart are the means?”, judged against the null world’s spread. F asks the same of four means at once: how spread out are they, compared with the noise inside each group?
  • The bottom line is the same yardstick δ used. In Week 6 we judged δ against the spread of the null world (the SE), which came from the scatter of the boreholes.
  • With exactly two groups, F = t²: same test (Exercise 2’s extension has students check this).

“Relative to within”

12:00

w08-f-ratio.R

  • Left is our data, the same sites as the last four slides. Right: the same four means, each site moved twice as far from its region’s mean.
  • Ask which panel you would be willing to call a real regional difference, before reading the F values.
  • Doubling the scatter divides F by four: the spread enters squared.
  • Both F values come from aov() on the panel’s own points.
  • Is 14.2 big? Compared to what? The next slide builds the comparison.

The null world for F

12:05

  • Type this, don’t paste it. It is Week 6’s shuffle, line for line, with F where δ was. Students have the data; race them.

  • Anticipated code:

    wind <- read.csv("data/wind_output.csv")
f_of <- function(region) {
  summary(aov(wind$capacity_pct ~ region))[[1]][["F value"]][1]
}
f_obs <- f_of(wind$region)
f_obs

f_of(sample(wind$region))

set.seed(7)
null_f <- replicate(1000, f_of(sample(wind$region)))

ggplot(data.frame(null_f), aes(x = null_f)) +
  geom_histogram(bins = 30) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = f_obs, colour = "red")

mean(null_f >= f_obs)

  • Numbers from a real run: F = 14.21. Shuffled F values huddle near 1; the largest of 1000 is 8.8, and the 95th percentile 2.8. None reaches 14.2, so the simulated p is below 1/1000.

  • One tail only: F can’t be negative, and only a big F is surprising.

  • The shortcut: summary(aov(capacity_pct ~ region, data = wind)) gives F(3, 96) = 14.21, p = 9.8 × 10⁻⁸. Say it, don’t type it: that line is Exercise 2.

  • “This is an ANOVA”, just as Week 6’s shuffle was a t-test.

Corrections for multiple comparisons

12:10
Method How When
Tukey’s HSD All pairwise, designed for ANOVA After a significant ANOVA
Bonferroni Divide α by number of tests Simple, conservative, any context

Both reduce the false positive rate by being stricter about what counts as significant.

  • Don’t go deep into the math. The key idea: when you test multiple things, you raise the bar for each individual test.

For ANOVA: η² (eta-squared)

12:28

The share of the variation explained by the groups: Week 7’s R², for a categorical predictor.

η² Interpretation
0.01 Small
0.06 Medium
0.14 Large

Always report both the p-value and an effect size.

A policy-maker needs to know “how big?” — not just “is it real?”

  • [12:28–12:33] Recall Week 6: “significant ≠ important”, and Cohen’s d for two groups. η² does the same job for several.
  • aov() is lm() with a categorical predictor: summary(lm(capacity_pct ~ region)) gives R² = η² = 0.31 for the wind data. Students calculate it on the application page.
  • Link to “Is that a big number?” from Week 2. Same habit, formalized.

Key points

12:38
  1. Multiple tests inflate false positives — correct for them
  2. ANOVA compares multiple groups in one test; TukeyHSD finds which differ
  3. Significance ≠ importance — always report effect sizes
  4. η² is R² for groups: how much of the variation they explain
