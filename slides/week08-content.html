How did you do?

The Economist, 23 September 2026: “Many surgical interventions are little better than placebo”.

Hands up: a row? Two rows? Full house?

Which square was hardest to fill?

  • [20 min; clock time TBC: Week 8 plan not yet rebalanced] Recap of Weeks 3–7 through the bias-bingo homework (materials/bias-bingo.md). Answer key with paragraph numbers: staff/bias-bingo-key.qmd.
  • Quick show of hands, then ask for the hardest square. Expect base rate neglect or multiple comparisons. Multiple comparisons has no clean example; reward anyone who argued “not here”.
  • Don’t read the article out; students have it. Quote only the phrases on the slides.

Why did surgeons think it worked?

Patients felt better after surgery. Three stories fit that:

  1. The operation fixed the problem
  2. The body healed on its own
  3. Being cared for made them feel better (placebo)

Pooling 100 surgical trials: about two-thirds of the improvement was stories 2 and 3.

  • Squares: confounding, placebo, effect size.
  • Recovery and ritual always travel with the operation, so an uncontrolled before/after comparison credits the scalpel with all three. That is confounding in its purest form.
  • “Is that a big number?” One-third of the benefit is still a benefit; the question is whether it justifies the risk and cost.
  • Ask: which square did you put this under? Several answers are right.

How do you separate them?

Compare Isolates
Surgery vs sham surgery the operation itself
Sham surgery vs no treatment the placebo
No treatment, before vs after natural recovery

One 2002 trial splashed water into a dish so the sham sounded right.

  • Squares: control group, randomization, blinding, “Compared to what?”.
  • Each row is a control group answering a different question. Most of the benefit in the sham arm turned out to be natural recovery, not placebo.
  • Moseley’s knee trial (165 patients): the team matched even sounds a patient under general anaesthetic might register. Blinding taken seriously.
  • A 2020 review: only about 1 % of trials for chronic musculoskeletal pain compared surgery with doing nothing at all.

Why were surgeons so sure?

  • Patients who come back for check-ups are mostly the ones who got better
  • Those who come back don’t want to disappoint their surgeon
  • Surgeons’ predictions of who will improve: no better than a coin toss
  • Squares: survivorship bias, reporting bias, confirmation bias.
  • The surgeon’s evidence is their own follow-up clinic. The failures have gone elsewhere; the successes are polite. Years of experience build confidence on a biased sample.
  • “Confidently be unconfident” (Blazeby) is a good line to leave on the screen verbally.

The square most people missed

Scans find tears and “abnormalities” in knees, backs and shoulders.

The same abnormalities are common in people with no pain at all.

How plausible was this before we tested?

  • Square: base rate neglect (Week 6).
  • We want P(abnormality causes pain | abnormality seen). If most pain-free people of the same age have it too, finding it in a patient tells you very little. Same structure as HolmesCo’s gold assay.
  • Also worth a minute: the paid-per-operation vs salaried contrast (UK shoulder operations fell by about four-fifths after the trials; US numbers did not). Is that a fair comparison? Countries differ in far more than how surgeons are paid: confounding again.

Now make it geological

What does each of these look like in a geological investigation?

Surgery Geology
Only recovered patients return Dry holes and failed slopes never get written up
Patients heal anyway Groundwater recovers once pumping stops. Did the remediation work?
Abnormal scans in painless knees Faults are everywhere. Is this one why the ground subsided?
Surgeons have no uncertainty Experts disagree on the same seismic section

In geology nobody says the word “placebo”. Would you spot these?

  • Ask for the geological versions students wrote at home first; reveal the table only as backup.
  • Seismic interpretation: Bond et al. (2007, GSA Today) gave the same synthetic section to hundreds of geoscientists; most did not reach the right interpretation.
  • Remediation is rarely compared with an untreated control site. The answer is the Week 5 before–after–control–impact design.
  • Link forward: a model is a claim about causes. Today’s question, “What is the model not capturing?”, is another way of asking which of these we’ve missed.

What is a model?

A deliberate simplification that captures some features of reality and ignores others.

Every statistical test you’ve run contains an implicit model.

The t-test models the data as two normal distributions, one per group.

Now we make the model explicit.

  • [20 min block; clock time TBC: Week 8 plan not yet rebalanced] Linear regression — concept and live demo.

Linear regression

\[y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \varepsilon\]

“y increases by β₁ for every one-unit increase in x, plus noise.”

That’s the whole model. It’s powerful — and limited.

Live demo

  • Type this, don’t paste it. The students have elec on their own page; tell them to beat you to the R² and start typing a minute in.

  • Anticipated code:

    elec <- read.csv("data/uk_electricity.csv")

ggplot(elec, aes(x = year, y = bioenergy_twh)) +
  geom_point() +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm")

model <- lm(bioenergy_twh ~ year, data = elec)
summary(model)

  • The slide previously showed lm(output ~ windspeed, data = turbine_df) and claimed R² ≈ 0.6. No such dataset exists in this repo, so no one could ever have run it. Biomass over time is real data the students already know from Week 1.

  • Numbers: slope 1.693 TWh per year, R² = 0.926, p = 4.4e-15, residual SE 3.73 on 24 df. “Biomass generation has risen about 1.7 TWh a year, and a straight line accounts for 93 % of the variation.”

  • Deliberately not the solar data — that is Exercise 1 in a few slides’ time, and its negative slope should surprise them.

Reading the output

Element Meaning
Intercept (β₀) Predicted y when x = 0
Slope (β₁) Change in y per unit change in x
R² Proportion of variance explained
p-value (slope) Is the slope distinguishable from zero?
  • Read it off the demo: “R² = 0.93 means 93 % of the variation is explained. 7 % is something else.”
  • The intercept is −3387 TWh: the line’s prediction for the year 0. Ask what it means. Nothing: it is where the line happens to cross x = 0, far outside the data.

The four assumptions

  1. Linearity — the relationship is actually linear
  2. Independence — residuals don’t influence each other
  3. Homoscedasticity — constant variance of residuals
  4. Normality of residuals (for inference)
  • [20 min block; clock time TBC: Week 8 plan not yet rebalanced] Assumptions, diagnostic plots, what to do when violated.

Diagnostic plots

Four plots, two that matter most:

Plot What to look for
Residuals vs Fitted Pattern = non-linearity or heteroscedasticity
QQ plot Departures from the line = non-normal residuals

  • Anticipated code — one line, and model is still in the session from the live demo a few slides back:

    plot(model)

  • If the session has been reset, re-run the demo cell first.

  • Walk through each plot. “No pattern in residuals vs fitted = good. Points on the QQ line = good.”

  • Biomass over time is a useful case here: R² is 0.93 and the residuals-vs-fitted panel still curves, because growth was flat then steep. High R², wrong shape — which is the point of the whole session.

What’s wrong?

w08-residuals.R

  • The residuals show a seasonal pattern — the model is missing a time-dependent structure
  • R² = 0.49 means 51 % of the variation is unexplained
  • “Dominant” is a stretch when your model misses more than it captures

Failure 1: Overfitting

A model that fits the training data perfectly but fails on new data.

It’s memorized the noise.

  • [25 min block; clock time TBC: Week 8 plan not yet rebalanced] Three failures — each a different form of hubris. Demo the polynomial overfitting live.

Demo: polynomial degree 15

The polynomial passes close to almost every point, and swings wildly between them. The straight line fits worse, but predicts better.

More complex ≠ more useful.

  • Type this, don’t paste it. Anticipated code (the same simulation as the next slide’s figure):

    set.seed(2801)
pts <- data.frame(x = sort(runif(20, 0, 10)))
pts$y <- 3 + 1.4 * pts$x + rnorm(20, 0, 2.5)

ggplot(pts, aes(x = x, y = y)) +
  geom_point() +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm", formula = y ~ x, se = FALSE) +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm", formula = y ~ poly(x, 15), se = FALSE,
              colour = "red", n = 400) +
  coord_cartesian(ylim = c(-5, 25))

  • The visual contrast is striking. coord_cartesian() keeps the data on screen when the polynomial leaves it.

  • The next slide is the same contrast, pre-rendered — use it as the fallback if WebR misbehaves, and leave it up while they discuss.

What that looks like

w08-overfitting.R

  • Both R² values are read off the fitted models, so “fits worse” is arithmetic. “Predicts better” is not measured on this figure: make it concrete with the question below.
  • Ask for a prediction at x = 11 from each model. One of them has an answer; the other has left the building.

Failure 2: Extrapolation

A model that works within the observed range but breaks down outside it.

Example: UK solar generation has grown roughly exponentially since 2010. Extrapolate the curve, and by 2035 solar alone exceeds all UK generation.

w08-extrapolation.R

What’s the model not capturing?

  • Take answers before offering any: planning constraints, grid limits, diminishing suitable sites.

HolmesCo’s extrapolation

⚒️ HolmesCo

Geological Consultants Since 2019

“Our Trends Hold Water”™

“Our linear trend in groundwater decline predicts the aquifer will be empty by 2028.”

What would you need to know before believing this?

  • Take answers first. The two that matter: seasonal recharge (a short record that ends in a dry spell exaggerates the decline), and whether the decline was caused by temporary pumping that will stop.

Failure 3: Blind spots

Things the model structurally cannot represent.

  • A single-turbine power curve has no term for wake interference → overpredicts wind farm output by 10–20 %
  • A carbon accounting model that counts biomass as zero structurally cannot see the emissions it ignores

You can only fix a blind spot if you know it’s there.

The new refrain

“What is the model not capturing?”

Overfitting: too much faith in your data.

Extrapolation: too much faith in your model.

Blind spots: too much faith in your framework.

  • These are three forms of the same mistake: confusing the map for the territory. Each is a different flavour of hubris.

Wrap-up

Key points

  1. A model is a simplification — powerful, but limited
  2. Always check the diagnostics: residuals don’t lie
  3. Three failures: overfitting, extrapolation, blind spots
  4. Always ask: “What is my model not capturing?”
