What are we trying to do?

11:10

Compress thousands of numbers into a few useful ones.

Three questions about any dataset:

  1. Where is the centre?
  2. How spread out is it?
  3. What shape is it?
  • [11:10–11:30] This is bread-and-butter descriptive statistics. Keep it practical: every concept gets a geoscience example.

Centre: mean vs median

11:13

Mean

The “balance point.”

Sensitive to extremes.

Pull one value way up → mean shifts.

Median

The middle value.

Robust to extremes.

Pull one value way up → median barely moves.

The Jacobite on Glenfinnan Viaduct: Eric Kilby, CC BY-SA 4.0

  • Example: Scottish income tax paid. Median £4k; mean £6k. J.K. Rowling £50M income tax/yr vs 5M Scots
  • She and a dependent move to England: no effect on median, big effect on mean.
  • Picturing the effect of a solo individual can help us to think about when the shape of a dataset may invite confusion.

When do they differ? When the distribution is skewed.

  • A tiny number of high earners pay much more income tax than everyone else put together
  • Same with earthquake magnitudes — a few huge quakes pull the mean up.

Spread

11:17
  • Range — max minus min
    • Sensitive to one extreme value
  • IQR — middle 50 % of the data
    • Robust to outliers
  • Standard deviation — the “typical” (root mean square) distance from the mean
  • Median absolute deviation – the median distance from the median

Another kind of spread: Bruno, Germany; CC0

Why spread matters

11:20

w02-spread.R

  • If a report only listed the means, both boreholes would appear identical.
  • “The borehole temperature was 15 °C” means something quite different in each case: would you stand by that statement in borehole B?

Shape

11:23

w02-distribution-shapes.R

  • Symmetric: mean ≈ median. Many natural processes.
  • Skewed: the mean chases the tail — income, grain size, ore grades. The long tail drags the mean away from the median. The median is the more “typical” value.
  • Bimodal: same mean and median but a very different distribution. Key message: a mean without a histogram can be deeply misleading.

The two-humped problem

11:27

Bactrian camels: two humps, one valley. Pixabay Content License

w02-bimodal.R

  • On the bimodal panel, the mean and median both land in the valley and describes almost none of the data.
  • One average cannot describe two populations.

Always look at the distribution, not just the mean.

  • The camel is the mnemonic: a bactrian has two humps, and the point between them is not where the camel is.
  • Real geoscience example: a sediment sample with two source areas, or a borehole set spanning two formations. The grand mean is an artefact.
  • Ask: “What would you report instead?” Answer: identify the groups first, then summarize each. This previews Week 5 on confounders.

Is that a big number?

11:33

Wood pellets: US Forest Service, public domain

UK bioenergy electricity: 41 TWh/year.

Is that a big number? Compared to what?

  • Ask: “What would be a sensible thing to compare this to?” Then throw back whichever of these fits:
    • Another country: the UK is the world’s 6th-largest bioenergy generator (after China, Brazil, Germany, the US, Japan). The US, which sends us ~¾ of our pellets, makes only a little more (46 TWh) with 5× our population: 1 % of its electricity, against our 14 %. France, a similar size: 10 TWh; Spain: 6 TWh.
    • Per person: ~580 kWh each in the UK; US 130, France 160, Germany 600, Finland 1850 (pulp and paper mills burn their waste).
    • Total UK generation (2025, DUKES) is ~294 TWh. So bioenergy ≈ 14 %. This is comparatively high, not top: Denmark 22 %, Luxembourg 29 %, Finland 13 %, Germany 10 %.
    • Other UK sources (2025): more than all UK nuclear (36 TWh); twice solar (20 TWh); half of wind (86 TWh); gas 93 TWh.
    • What it could power: at 2500 kWh a home, ~16 million homes: over half of the UK’s 28.6 million households (on paper).
    • One station: Drax alone made 15.0 TWh: over a third of it, 6 % of UK power, ~6 million homes’ worth.
    • Over time: 4.3 TWh in 2000; it has grown tenfold.
  • Sources: Ember Yearly Electricity Data (2024–25; downloaded 2026-10-12), with Our World in Data populations (2024); data/uk_electricity.csv (DUKES 2026 Table 5.6.B); data/pellet_imports.csv (US 74 % of 2024 imports); ONS Families and households in the UK: 2024; Drax Full year results 2025 (26 February 2026).

Is that a big number?

11:36

Drax power station: © Drax

Drax power station emits 14 Mt CO₂/year.

Is that a big number? Compared to what?

  • UK CO₂ emissions: ~285 Mt in 2025; 367 MtCO₂e of greenhouse gases . Drax (a single station) is ~5 % of that – though not included (!) in the 285 Mt figure.

  • Per person: UK CO₂ is ~4.1 t per person (284.8 Mt ÷ 69 million). Drax emits as much as 3.4 million Britons.

  • 3M dairy cows: or almost half the UK’s cattle (dairy & beef)

Is that a big number?

11:39

United Downs well pad, 2019, before the plant was built: Jonathan Wilkins / Geograph, CC BY-SA 2.0

The UK’s geothermal power station: 3 MW.

Is that a big number? Compared to what?

  • United Downs, near Redruth, Cornwall: switched on February 2026. Water from a 5 km well, the deepest and hottest in the UK, at over 185 °C. Run flat out, 3 MW for a year is ~25 GWh.
  • Comparators to throw back:
    • All UK electricity (294 TWh): about 0.01 %, or 1 part in 12 000. Too small a percentage to be a sensible comparator: that is the point.
    • All UK geothermal electricity Deep geothermal heat is a few more MW (Eden Project 1.4 MW, Gateshead 6 MW). Shallow ground-source heat pumps are the big one: ~55 000 of them, 847 MW, ~1430 GWh of heat a year (2023), roughly 60× United Downs. Ask: is the ground’s heat a few metres down geothermal, or stored sunshine?
    • Homes: ~10 000 homes’ electricity (the developer’s own figure; 25 GWh ÷ 2500 kWh agrees).
    • Per person: split across the UK, ~0.4 kWh each a year: about four kettles (3 kW for 2 min ≈ 0.1 kWh).
    • Drax: Drax’s biomass makes in about 15 hours what United Downs makes in a year (15.0 TWh ÷ 8760 h ≈ 1.7 GWh an hour).
    • A data centre: one building of the planned QTS campus at Cambois, Northumberland, is rated at 72 MW: 24 United Downs. The whole campus (1.1 GW) would need ~370.
    • A swimming pool: Penzance’s Jubilee Pool lido has been heated from a 410 m geothermal well since 2020; the pool says ~80 % of its heat is geothermal. Its heat pumps are rated ~480 kW (the installer’s figure): a sixth of United Downs’ 3 MW, and heat, not electricity. Flat out all year that is ≤4.2 GWh of heat; at a more likely third to half of that, ~1.4–2.1 GWh, the gas heating of ~150–220 homes (9500 kWh each).
    • Closer to home (heat): Gateshead’s mine-water heat network, 6 MW of heat from flooded coal workings, is the UK’s largest. Seaham Garden Village is building one from Dawdon mine water for 750 homes. Under Weardale, the Eastgate borehole (2004) hit brine at 46 °C at 995 m in granite.
    • Other countries: geothermal is 29 % of Iceland’s electricity (5.6 TWh, ~220 United Downs) and 45 % of Kenya’s (6.0 TWh).
  • Sources: Envirotec, “Geothermal electricity generation commences at United Downs” (2 March 2026); Drax Full year results 2025; DatacenterDynamics, “QTS files to build 1.1 GW data center campus in Northumberland”; Jubilee Pool, “Geothermal pool” (jubileepool.co.uk); Thermal Earth (installer), “Jubilee Pools, Cornwall”; IEA Geothermal 2023 UK country report (BGS/DESNZ, October 2024: Table 1 and §1); Ofgem medium gas TDCV (9500 kWh, from July 2026); Mining Remediation Authority (Seaham); Manning et al. (2007), J. Geol. Soc. 164: 371–382 (Eastgate); Ember Yearly Electricity Data 2024 (“other renewables”, nearly all geothermal there).

Why distributions matter

11:45

A histogram shows you what the mean and SD cannot:

  • Is the data symmetric or skewed?
  • Are there outliers?
  • Is there more than one cluster?

  • [11:45–12:05] Deeper dive into distributions. Connect to geoscience.

  • Open with: to know “is this a big number”, we need to know how our data are distributed. 1, 2, 3 SDs from the mean = 68 %, 95 %, 99.7 %

The normal distribution

11:49

The original bell curves: Pexels License

Ring a bell? Arises when many small, independent effects combine.

Central Limit Theorem (informally): averages of large samples tend toward normal, even if the underlying data aren’t.

Allows many statistical tests to assume normality, and carry on working even when raw data are messy.

  • Don’t go deep into CLT. The intuition is enough: “averages smooth things out.”

Log-normal distributions in geoscience

11:53

Shap Granite: jtweedie1976 / Flickr, CC BY 2.0

Many geological measurements are log-normal: a long tail of large values.

  • Grain size
  • Permeability
  • Ore grades
  • Trace-element concentrations

When data are log-normal, the mean can be very different from the typical value.

  • The next slide is this claim as a picture: most of the rock in single figures of mD (millidarcy - unit of permeability), a tail into the thousands, and a mean well above almost every sample.
  • Connection to “Is that a big number?”: if the data are log-normal, the mean is a poor summary. The median or geometric mean is better.
  • Why ore grades are log-normal: geochemical enrichment works multiplicatively — each hydrothermal pulse or fractionation event multiplies the existing concentration by some factor. A chain of multiplications is equivalent to a sum of logs, and sums tend toward normal (CLT), so log(concentration) is normal → concentration is log-normal. Grain size gets there the same way: each breakage event leaves a random fraction of the grain, a chain of multiplications (Kolmogorov 1941). Permeability scales with pore-throat size squared, times other factors that multiply, so it inherits the skew.
  • Earthquake magnitudes are skewed too but are not log-normal: the magnitude is already a logarithm, and it falls off exponentially (Gutenberg–Richter). Don’t list them here if asked.

What that looks like

11:57

w02-log-normal.R

  • The mean is larger than 85 % of the samples. It is not a typical permeability — it is not close to any of the rock. That is what “represents nobody” means.
  • The left panel is cut at the 99th percentile, and says so in the caption. Worth naming aloud: the honest way to crop a chart is to tell the reader you did.

When to log-transform

12:01

If your data:

  • Are positive (no zeros or negatives)
  • Span several orders of magnitude
  • Are right-skewed

Then log() often makes them more symmetric and easier to analyse.

Reach for log if you find yourself asking “how many times more”.

Leave it on the shelf when absolute (contra relative) differences are interesting.

  • This comes back in Week 6 (t-test on log-transformed data) and Week 8 (regression diagnostics).

What a good figure does

How a good figure works

12:07

The CRARP principles of graphic design:

  • Contrast: Make the story stand out.
  • Repetition: Consistent style between figures.
  • Alignment: Same scale, same zero, no perspective.
  • Repetition: Consistent style between figures.
  • Proximity: Position your annotations; compare neighbours.
  • Graphic designers summarize layout as CRAP — Contrast, Repetition, Alignment, Proximity (after Robin Williams, The Non-Designer’s Design Book; framing via Andrew Heiss, Data Visualization with R, session 2, https://datavizsp26.classes.andrewheiss.com/content/02-content.html).

The grammar of graphics

12:23 
ggplot(data, aes(x = ..., y = ..., colour = ...)) +
  geom_point() +   # geometry
  labs(...)         # labels

Data + Aesthetics + Geometries = a plot.

Data: A plot is just one way of displaying the data. Analyses don’t start with “I’d like to see a bar chart”, but with “What do these data tell me?”

Aesthetics: Translation layer: Which variable controls which visual channel? Channels: x, y, colour, plotting symbol…

Establishes the rules – doesn’t plot anything itself.

Geometries: What physical mark displays the visual channels?

Everything in ggplot2 follows this pattern.

  • This is the mental model. Once they have this, they can build any plot by looking up the right geom_*().

  • Claus Wilke, Fundamentals of Data Visualization — https://clauswilke.com/dataviz/. Start with Common pitfalls of color use.
  • Kieran Healy, Data Visualization: A Practical Introduction — https://socviz.co/. Written around ggplot2.
  • Cédric Scherer, “The evolution of a ggplot” — https://www.cedricscherer.com/2019/05/17/the-evolution-of-a-ggplot-ep.-1/. One default chart, polished step by step.
  • Andrew Heiss, Data Visualization with R — https://datavizsp26.classes.andrewheiss.com/. A full course; its sessions on graphic design, mapping data to graphics, amounts and proportions, and themes follow on from today.
