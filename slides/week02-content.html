Summarising data

🎓 Concept block 1

What are we trying to do?

Compress thousands of numbers into a few useful ones.

Three questions about any dataset:

  1. Where is the centre?
  2. How spread out is it?
  3. What shape is it?
  • [20 min block] This is bread-and-butter descriptive statistics. Keep it practical: every concept gets a geoscience example.

Centre: mean vs median

Mean

The “balance point.”

Sensitive to extremes.

Pull one value way up → mean shifts.

Median

The middle value.

Robust to extremes.

Pull one value way up → median barely moves.

When do they differ? When the distribution is skewed.

  • Example: UK household income. Mean is pulled up by high earners; median is more “typical.” Same with earthquake magnitudes — a few huge quakes pull the mean up.

Spread

  • Standard deviation — average distance from the mean
  • IQR — middle 50% of the data (robust to outliers)
  • Range — max minus min (sensitive to one extreme value)

Spread matters as much as centre. A mean of 10 with SD of 1 is very different from a mean of 10 with SD of 50.

  • Geoscience example: two boreholes both average 15°C, but one varies ±0.5°C and the other ±5°C. Very different stories.

Why spread matters

Figure 1

w02-spread.R

  • The means are identical, so a report quoting only the mean would describe these two boreholes with the same sentence.
  • Ask: which one would you drill next to? The answer is about spread, and there is no way to reach it from a table of means.

Shape

Figure 2

w02-distribution-shapes.R

Skewed: the mean chases the tail — income, grain size, ore grades.

  • Symmetric: mean ≈ median. Many natural processes.
  • Skewed: the long tail drags the mean away from the median. The median is the more “typical” value.
  • Bimodal: save this one — it sets up the next slide.
  • Key message: a mean without a histogram can be deeply misleading.

The two-humped problem

On the bimodal panel, the mean and median both land in the valley — a value that describes almost none of the data.

Two populations. Report one average and you have described neither.

Camelus bactrianus — PhyloPic, CC0

Always look at the distribution, not just the mean.

  • The camel is the mnemonic: a bactrian has two humps, and the point between them is not where the camel is.
  • Real geoscience example: a sediment sample with two source areas, or a borehole set spanning two formations. The grand mean is an artefact.
  • Ask: “What would you report instead?” Answer: identify the groups first, then summarise each. This previews Week 5 on confounders.

“Is that a big number?”

💬✏️ Exercise 1

The More or Less principle

Tim Harford, who presents BBC Radio 4’s More or Less.
PopTech, CC BY-SA 2.0

A number without a comparator is meaningless.

  • [15 min] This is the first key refrain. It should become a running question for the rest of the course.
  • Named after the BBC Radio 4 programme More or Less, which interrogates statistics in the news.

Try these

UK biomass electricity: 38 TWh/year.

Big or small? Compared to what?

Total UK electricity is ~320 TWh. So biomass ≈ 12%.

  • Present without context first. Let students guess. Then reveal the comparator.

Try these

Drax power station emits 12 Mt CO₂/year.

UK total CO₂: ~340 Mt. Drax is ~3.5% of the national total — from one building.

Try these

A single wind turbine produces about 6 GWh/year.

A UK household uses ~3,500 kWh/year. One turbine ≈ 1,700 homes.

The habit

Every time you see a number in this module, ask:

“Is that a big number?”

And its companion: “Compared to what?”

  • Bookmark this moment. Return to these phrases in every subsequent week. By Week 10, students should be asking this unprompted.

Data visualisation

🎓💻 Concept block 2

What makes a good figure?

  1. Show the data — don’t just summarise
  2. Make comparisons easy — same axes, same scales
  3. Don’t distort — honest axes, honest encodings
  4. Label clearly — axes, units, captions
  • [20 min block] Principles first, then live ggplot2 demo.

Bad figures

  • Truncated y-axes
  • Dual y-axes
  • 3D bar charts
  • Pie charts for non-compositional data
  • Cherry-picked date ranges
  • Misleading area encodings
  • Missing labels or units
  • Colour that obscures rather than reveals

Four of these, right now. Three more in the exercise.

  • [6 min for the four pairs that follow] Two minutes each at most: show the bad chart, take one guess from the room, advance to the fixed version. The “Spot the lie” exercise later does the slower version with truncated axes, cherry-picked ranges and pie charts.
  • Each pair is the same numbers drawn twice. Say that out loud: no data was changed between the two slides.
  • Missing labels and units is the one rule with no pair here — it gets a before/after of its own in Week 4 and again in Week 10.

The dual axis

Figure 3

w02-dual-axis-bad.R

  • Ask: “Is the wood being burnt less efficiently every year?” That is what the chart appears to say.
  • Then ask what each axis starts at. The left starts at 0; the right starts at 6000 kt.
  • Nobody chose those two ranges together. That is the whole problem: a dual axis lets you pick the relationship you want to show.

The dual axis, fixed

Figure 4

w02-dual-axis-good.R

Fix: put both series on one scale. Indexed to 2015, imports and generation move together — which is what you would expect if the pellets are being burnt at a roughly constant efficiency.

  • TWh and kt cannot share an axis, so index them: divide by the first year and multiply by 100. Now both axes are “percent of 2015”.
  • The divergence in the first chart was manufactured entirely by the choice of axis ranges.

The 3D bar chart

Figure 5

w02-fake-3d-bad.R

  • Take a show of hands on gas versus wind before advancing. The room will say wind.
  • The perspective shrink is 10% per step back. The real gaps are 4% (gas over wind) and under 1% (nuclear over bioenergy), so the decoration is larger than the data.

The 3D bar chart, fixed

Figure 6

w02-fake-3d-good.R

Fix: no third dimension — there is no third variable. Where the gap is smaller than the ink, print the numbers.

  • Both rankings flip. A decorative choice reversed the answer to the question the chart was drawn to answer.
  • General rule: a third dimension is only honest if a third variable is being encoded. Depth for its own sake is noise added to a signal.

The area encoding

Figure 7

w02-area-encoding-bad.R

  • Ask for the impression first: roughly how much have imports grown? The room will say “doubled”, because the ink did.
  • The side is proportional to the value, so the area goes as the square: 1.42 across becomes 2.0 in area.
  • This is the standard infographic sin. It shows up with circles, tree icons and stacks of little power stations.

The area encoding, fixed

Figure 8

w02-area-encoding-good.R

Fix: encode with length from a common baseline. It is the one channel the eye reads accurately — and it comes with an axis to check.

  • If a pictogram is unavoidable, scale the area: side proportional to the square root of the value. Better still, don’t.
  • Note what the honest version gains beyond honesty: an axis, so a reader can extract a number rather than an impression.

Colour that obscures

Figure 9

w02-colour-obscures-bad.R

  • Ask: which origin stopped supplying the UK altogether? Give them ten seconds of squinting at the legend.
  • Rainbow hues are not ordered and not equally distinguishable. Red and green in the same chart also defeats the commonest colour vision deficiency — about 1 in 12 men.
  • Every series got a colour, so no series got emphasis.

Colour that reveals

Figure 10

w02-colour-obscures-good.R

Fix: colour the story, grey the context, label on the line. Colour is a scarce resource — spend it where the reader should look.

  • Same six series, nothing dropped. The grey lines still carry the context; they just stop competing.
  • Russia’s series ends after 2021, and the 2022 gap is filled from the USA and Latvia. That is visible here and invisible in the rainbow.
  • Direct labels beat a legend: no eye-travel, and no memory test.

Live demo: building a ggplot

  • Live code this. ggplot2 is already attached on this slide, and elec is loaded by the starter code. Build it step by step:
    1. Start with geom_point() only
    2. Add geom_line()
    3. Add proper labels: labs(x = "Year", y = "Generation (TWh)")
    4. Map colour to fuel type (requires reshaping — show briefly)
  • Keep to 3–4 builds. Narrate the “grammar”: data + aesthetics + geometries.

The grammar of graphics

ggplot(data, aes(x = ..., y = ..., colour = ...)) +
  geom_point() +   # geometry
  labs(...)         # labels

Data + Aesthetics + Geometries = a plot.

Data: A plot is just one way of displaying the data. Analyses don’t start with “I’d like to see a bar chart”, but with “What do these data tell me?”

Aesthetics: Translation layer: Which variable controls which visual channel? Channels: x, y, colour, plotting symbol…

Establishes the rules – doesn’t plot anything itself.

Geometries: What physical mark displays the visual channels?

Everything in ggplot2 follows this pattern.

  • This is the mental model. Once they have this, they can build any plot by looking up the right geom_*().

Spot the lie

✏️ Exercise 2

Instructions

I’ll show you some deliberately misleading charts.

For each one: what’s wrong, and how would you fix it?

  • [12 min] Quick-fire. Show each chart, give 60 seconds to think, then discuss — the next slide is always the fixed version, so hold it back until they have committed to an answer. This previews Week 4’s “how to lie with statistics” in a lighter form.
  • Could use a poll (Mentimeter/hands) for speed.

Chart 1: The truncated axis

Figure 11

w02-truncated-axis.R

  • Let the chart sit for a moment. First reaction: “Generation is collapsing!” Then ask: what does the y-axis start at?
  • Don’t answer it here — advance to the fixed version.

Chart 1, fixed

Figure 12

w02-truncated-axis-good.R

Fix: Start the y-axis at zero for bar charts. The bar’s length encodes the ratio, so a non-zero baseline breaks the encoding.

  • The y-axis in the previous slide began at 270 TWh. The actual drop from 2019 to 2024 is about 13% — noticeable, but not the catastrophe the chart implied.
  • Rule: bar lengths encode ratios. A non-zero baseline breaks that encoding. Line charts can sometimes justify a non-zero start, but bars never can.

Chart 2: The cherry-picked date range

Figure 13

w02-cherry-picked-range.R

  • Three points, three years, a confident downward line. Ask what a reader would need to see to check it.

Chart 2, fixed

Figure 14

w02-cherry-picked-range-good.R

Fix: Show the full time range. Bioenergy grew from 4 TWh (2000) to 40 TWh (2024) — a tenfold increase hidden by that window.

  • The dip is real: 2021–2023 genuinely falls. Cherry-picking does not require fake data, only a well-chosen window.
  • Shading the window rather than deleting it makes the point stick — the lie is visible inside the truth.
  • Ask: how would you decide, honestly, where a series should start? Best answer: at the start of the data, unless you can say why not.

Chart 3: The misleading pie chart

Figure 15

w02-double-counted-pie.R

  • Ask: “How many slices include wind energy?” Answer: two (Wind and Low carbon). Same for nuclear, solar, and bioenergy.
  • The total shown is ~446 TWh; actual UK generation is 285 TWh.
  • General rule: pie chart slices must be mutually exclusive and sum to a meaningful 100%. If they don’t, use a bar chart.

Chart 3, fixed

Figure 16

w02-double-counted-pie-good.R

Fix: “Low carbon” double-counted Wind + Nuclear + Solar + Bioenergy, so the slices summed to 156%. An overlapping category is a shading, not a slice — and bars are sortable, labelable, and they add up.

  • “Other” is total generation minus the named fuels — computed, not asserted, so every TWh in the table is accounted for.
  • Note what the bar chart gains: you can read gas against wind directly, which is nearly impossible on a pie.
  • Pies are defensible for two or three parts of a genuine whole. Past that, bars.

Distributions

🎓 Concept block 3

Why distributions matter

A histogram shows you what the mean and SD cannot:

  • Is the data symmetric or skewed?
  • Are there outliers?
  • Is there more than one cluster?
  • [20 min block] Deeper dive into distributions. Connect to geoscience.

The normal distribution

The familiar bell curve. Appears when many small, independent effects combine.

Central Limit Theorem (informally): averages of large samples tend toward normal, even if the underlying data aren’t.

This is why so many statistical tests assume normality — and why they often work even when the raw data are messy.

  • Don’t go deep into CLT. The intuition is enough: “averages smooth things out.”

Log-normal distributions in geoscience

Many geological measurements are log-normal: skewed right, with a long tail of large values.

  • Grain size
  • Permeability
  • Ore grades
  • Earthquake magnitudes

When data are log-normal, the mean can be very different from the typical value.

  • The next slide is this claim as a picture: most of the rock in single figures of mD (millidarcy - unit of permeability), a tail into the thousands, and a mean well above almost every sample.
  • Connection to “Is that a big number?”: if the data are log-normal, the mean is a poor summary. The median or geometric mean is better.
  • Why ore grades are log-normal: geochemical enrichment works multiplicatively — each hydrothermal pulse or fractionation event multiplies the existing concentration by some factor. A chain of multiplications is equivalent to a sum of logs, and sums tend toward normal (CLT), so log(concentration) is normal → concentration is log-normal. Contrast with grain size and permeability, where it is energy averaging over many transport or flow events that produces the log-normal; the mechanism differs but the mathematics is the same.

What that looks like

Figure 17

w02-log-normal.R

  • The mean is larger than 85% of the samples. It is not a typical permeability — it is not close to any of the rock. That is what “represents nobody” means.
  • The left panel is cut at the 99th percentile, and says so in the caption. Worth naming aloud: the honest way to crop a chart is to tell the reader you did.

When to log-transform

If your data:

  • Are positive (no zeros or negatives)
  • Span several orders of magnitude
  • Are right-skewed

Then log() often makes them more symmetric and easier to analyse.

  • This comes back in Week 6 (t-test on log-transformed data) and Week 8 (regression diagnostics).
