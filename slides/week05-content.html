Controls

11:20

What you hold constant so you can isolate the variable of interest.

Example: How does latitude affect wind farm output?

What else differs between wind farms at different latitudes, apart from latitude?

Here I’m thinking on the scale of the UK. But could think more broadly too.

Control for Why
Turbine model Different models have different capacities
Terrain Ridgetop/valley/offshore: affects wind speed
Grid curtailment Grid operator tells certain farms to stop generating

Harder to control: Weather; maintenance schedules; …

  • Geoscience angle: in field sciences we rarely get true experiments. We observe natural variation and try to isolate factors. This makes design more important, not less.
  • Maintenance schedules: more efficient after a service? Lack the data to be able to control for this - operators won’t tell you.

Treatment vs control groups

11:25

Lab sciences

Treatment group gets the intervention.

Control group doesn’t.

Random assignment.

Field sciences

“Treatment” is often a natural condition.

“Control” is a comparison group.

You can’t randomize geology.

  • Because we can’t randomize, we need to think harder about what might confound our comparisons. That’s next.

What is a confounder?

11:45

A variable that is correlated with both the treatment and the outcome.

You can’t tell which caused the effect.

  • [11:45–12:15] Confounders are the hidden trap in observational studies.

Confounding examples

Ice cream by the sea: Hatice Baran / Pexels

More solar panels → higher GDP Solar causes wealth? Latitude, governance, investment

Confounders, contra causation, can cause correlation.

Wind farms and birds

11:50

w05-wind-map.R

  • Real data, and a real question: do wind farms harm birds? Every square of Great Britain that is mostly land. Wind farm = 8 or more turbines, from the government’s Renewable Energy Planning Database.
  • Birds: every species recorded in each square in 2006–15, from the British Trust for Ornithology’s volunteer records (Garden BirdWatch, BirdTrack and surveys), via the NBN Atlas.
  • Ask where the wind farms are: exposed, windy, away from towns. Park that.

Wind farm squares have fewer species

11:52

Turbines harm birds?

# Wind farm by 2015, or not
squares$farm <- squares$largest_farm_by_2015 >= 8

# What am I measuring? Bird species recorded, 2006–15
ggplot(squares, aes(x = farm, y = species)) +
  stat_summary(fun.data = mean_se,            # mean and
               fun.args = list(mult = 1.96))  # 95 % CI

Then hone:

# At top:
theme_set(theme_grey(base_size = 18))

# Replace sq$farm:
ifelse(squares$largest_farm_by_2015 >= 8,
                       "Wind farm", "None")
  • Output: no wind farm 134.5 species (95 % CI 132.8–136.2, 2100 squares); wind farm 126.3 (120.8–131.8, 139 squares). About 8 fewer.
  • The next five boxes each rebuild squares in a hidden setup block, so any of them runs on its own: you can jump in mid-deck.
  • mean_se(mult = 1.96) is a normal-approximation CI; the polished script uses the t quantile. Same numbers to one decimal place.
  • Let the room agree that turbines harm birds. It is the story they expect, and the confidence intervals do not overlap.
  • Then ask: where do birdwatchers live? Who submits these records?

A possible confounder: recording effort

11:54

  • A confounder must be tied to both sides. Check the diagnosis before treating it. This slide: the outcome. Next slide: the exposure.

  • cor(): One number for how closely two columns rise together: 1 in lockstep, 0 no relation, −1 one falls as the other rises.

  • Anticipated code:

    cor(squares$records, squares$species) # Pearson coefficient = 0.71
# What have I done wrong?
# ALWAYS LOOK AT THE DATA FIRST!
ggplot(squares, aes(x = records, y = species)) + geom_point()
cor(squares$records, squares$species, method = "spearman") # 0.82

  • Outputs: Pearson 0.71, Spearman 0.82. The scatter curves: species climb fast, then level off. Spearman uses ranks, so it doesn’t need a straight line. Busier squares list more species: tied to the outcome.

A possible confounder: recording effort

11:57

  • tapply(): Do it the long way first, one group at a time, then: “But there’s a shortcut!” tapply(x, group, f) splits x by group and applies f to each piece. (In Python: groupby().agg().)

  • Anticipated code:

    ggplot(squares, aes(x = farm, y = records)) + geom_violin()
median(squares$records[squares$farm == "Wind farm"])
median(squares$records[squares$farm == "None"])

# the shortcut
tapply(squares$records, squares$farm, median)

  • The violins are squashed flat by a few city squares with hundreds of thousands of records. + scale_y_log10() shows the shapes, if wanted. That skew is also why median, not mean.

  • Median records: wind farm 5130, no wind farm 11 456. Wind farm squares are looked at half as hard. Tied to both: a confounder.

Allowing for recording effort: Stratification

11:59

  • The method is stratification: put squares into ten bands by number of records; compare each square with its own band.

  • New in R: quantile(x, 0:10 / 10) gives the cut points that split x into ten equal-sized groups; cut() puts each square in its band. ave(x, group) is tapply()’s cousin: the group average, repeated for every row, so it lines up with x.

  • Anticipated code:

    # 1.
squares$band <- cut(squares$records,
                    quantile(squares$records, 0:10 / 10),
                    include.lowest = TRUE, dig.lab = 6)
table(squares$band)  # about 224 squares in each

# 2.
squares$vs_band <- squares$species -
  ave(squares$species, squares$band)

  • dig.lab = 6 prints the band edges as plain numbers (1176, not 1.18e+03). Leave it out first if you want to show why it’s there.

  • Bands run from 158–1176 records to 52 207–311 015: about 224 squares in each.

  • A square with ten thousand garden-feeder records will list more species than a moor that three people visited. Records stand in for effort.

Before the turbines

12:03

Polished version: w05-birds-before.R

The “benefit” was there before any turbine. Something else chose those squares.

  • Narrate the logic first: if turbines caused the gap, it cannot predate them.

  • Anticipated code:

    # 1.
untouched <- squares[!squares$any_wind_by_2015, ]

# 2.
untouched$later <- ifelse(untouched$largest_farm_later >= 8,
                          "Wind farm later", "Never")

# 3.
ggplot(untouched, aes(x = later, y = vs_band)) +
  stat_summary(fun.data = mean_se,
               fun.args = list(mult = 1.96))

  • The effort bands were set on all squares on the last slide, before this subset: the measure is the same one. Worth saying if asked.

  • Output: never 1711 squares, −1.4 (−2.5 to −0.3); wind farm later 238 squares, +3.7 (1.2 to 6.2). A gap of about +5, before any turbine.

  • These squares had no turbines while the birds were being recorded, so turbines cannot have caused the gap. Developers pick sites that were already better for birds: windy, open, unenclosed, away from people.

  • “Later” includes refused applications: they still show where developers chose to go.

  • Correcting for one confounder changed the answer; it did not make the answer right. Back to the table: the “real cause” for row three is both recording effort and site selection.

What would a controlled experiment look like?

12:06

You fund wildlife research. Design the study that would tell you what wind farms do to birds.

  • [5 min, desks, within 11:45–12:15] Hoped-for answers: count birds at the same site before and after; compare with similar sites where nothing was built; count the same way every time.
  • You cannot randomize where wind farms go — so this is the best that field science can do.

Same sites, before and after

12:08

Curlew: Odd Rune Falch / Pexels

Pearce-Higgins et al. (2012) J. Appl. Ecol. 49: 386–394

Design

  • 18 upland wind farms, UK
  • Breeding birds counted before, during and after construction
  • Same counts on nearby reference sites with no turbines

Findings

  • Snipe −53 % during construction
  • Curlew about −40 %, with no recovery
  • No such declines on reference sites
  • Each wind farm is compared with itself, which removes site selection; the reference sites catch changes that would have happened anyway. This is a before–after–control–impact design.
  • Skylark and stonechat increased: “may be spurious” (differences between wind farm and reference sites). But is this confirmation bias? Even a good design has to watch for confounders.
  • Most of the damage was during construction, not operation. A snapshot map lacks that granularity.
  • doi:10.1111/j.1365-2664.2012.02110.x

Why the reference sites?

12:10

⚒️ HolmesCo

Geological Consultants Since 2019

“Results Well Above Average”™

Remediation Results

Last summer we treated the five wells with the highest nitrate in the catchment. This summer, all five are lower. Treatment works.

What would those five wells have done without treatment?

Regression to the mean: pick sites because they were extreme, and they look better next time anyway. An extreme reading is partly bad luck, and luck doesn’t repeat.

  • [4 min, within 11:45–12:15] Desks, one minute, then reveal.
  • Hoped-for answer: fallen anyway. Last summer’s top five were partly high because of that summer: a dry spell, a fertilizer application, a sampling day after rain. Next year’s draw is ordinary.
  • Not a confounder, but the same cure: a comparison group selected the same way. Five equally bad wells, left untreated, show how much improvement comes for free. That is what Pearce-Higgins’s reference sites do.
  • It runs the other way too: the best sites slip back. The top of any league table (wettest catchment, windiest turbine, best borehole) is partly luck.
  • Geoscience versions: rockfall netting after the worst year on record; flood defences after a record flood; “our intervention” credited with a return to normal.

How to handle confounders

12:13
  1. Randomization — when possible (rare in geoscience)
  2. Stratification — compare within subgroups
  3. Matching — pair similar observations
  4. Acknowledgement — when all else fails, be honest about what you can’t control
  • 1: e.g. “Choose at random which requested turbine sites we’ll build on”
  • 2: e.g. stratify by n bird observations, earlier
  • 3: Compare sites where confounders happen to have the same values
  • 4: most common in geoscience and policy analysis. Honesty about confounders is itself a research skill.

Sampling strategies

🎓 Concept block 3

  • Design sketch
  • Wrap-up

Our objective is to understand a target “population”. In biology, that might be every ant in a colony. In Earth Sciences, we might want to characterize every crystal in a magma body. In Esh Spinning, we want complete knowledge of the subsurface geology across the site.

We’re seldom going to characterize the complete population, so we need a subsample. But how do we obtain a sample that has the same characteristics as the whole population?

How to choose your sample

12:15

Shap Granite. Sample a grain: which one? jtweedie1976 / Flickr, CC BY 2.0

Strategy How When
Random Every unit has equal probability Gold standard; often impractical
Stratified Sample within each subgroup Ensures representation
Systematic Every nth unit Regular grids, monitoring stations
Convenience Whatever’s easiest HolmesCo’s default

  • [12:15–12:40] Practical sampling. The key message: your sampling strategy is a decision you must justify.

  • Characterizing a magma body. Could: sample grains at random. Have to be careful: if we just point at a sample, we’re more likely to hit a big grain. That might be good if we want to characterize the composition - or bad if we want to characterize grain size!

  • Stratified: Sample all plag grains, all qz, all micas…

  • Systematic: A grid?

  • Random and systematic are the pair the table cannot separate: both are “unbiased”, and they look nothing alike. Note the gaps in the random panel — that is what stratification exists to prevent.
  • Systematic: Regular spacing is a shorthand for “pick the samples that are most different from one another” – R package Coreset. Difference may not be “distance in XY”.
  • The convenience panel is HolmesCo’s default: “we sampled these because they were there”. Honest, but weak. Ask what question that sample can answer, and about what area.

One sample, or many?

12:32

HolmesCo tested 36 core specimens from 3 boreholes.

Is that n = 36, or n = 3?

A replicate is one independent sample of the thing you are asking about. For “what is the site like?”, that thing is the ground, and specimens from one hole share its ground: n = 3.

Count them as 36 and the confidence interval shrinks for free. That is pseudoreplication.

Your sampling strategy, and your unit of replication, are decisions you must justify in your report.

  • [5 min, within 12:15–12:40] Ask the room before revealing. Some will say “it depends on the question”, which is the right answer: for “how variable is the rock within BH-01?”, 12 specimens are 12 replicates.
  • Geoscience versions: 100 grains from one thin section; hourly output from one turbine as evidence about turbines in a region; ten readings from one gauge on one day.
  • Sets up Week 6: “independence” is the first assumption of the t-test.
  • Research plan question 4 now asks each group for its unit of replication. Students will need to explain why they chose their data sources and whether their sample is representative.
