Has July warmed?

16:12

t.test(july, july_early)

    Welch Two Sample t-test

data:  july and july_early
t = 1.2445, df = 57.503, p-value = 0.2184
alternative hypothesis: true difference in means is not equal to 0
95 percent confidence interval:
 -0.3124955  1.3391622
sample estimates:
mean of x mean of y
 20.25000  19.73667
  • What is the null hypothesis?
  • What does p = 0.22 mean?
  • What does the confidence interval tell you that p doesn’t?
  • So: has July warmed?
  • [16:12–16:17]
  • This is the real world. There is only one real world. The p-value asks: How often would we see what we saw in the real world, if the real world was a null world?
  • The output: Welch t = 1.24, df = 57.5, p = 0.22. Difference 0.51 °C, 95 % CI [−0.31, 1.34] °C.
  • Null hypothesis: mean July maximum the same in both periods.
  • p = 0.22: ^ ^ ^
  • The interval runs from slight cooling to 1.3 °C of warming. The data can’t tell “no change” from substantial warming: not detected is not no warming. Is half a degree a big number? Compared to what? A single July varies by about 1.6 °C (standard deviation) from year to year: half a degree is lost in that noise with 30 Julys a side.
  • The non-whole df is Welch at work, as in the content session.
  • t.test(july, july_early) is the same test as t.test(y ~ group, data = df): two vectors instead of one column split by another. Recent period first, so the difference is positive.
  • Compared to what? Every month but June and July shows warming at p < 0.02. That’s the extension, next slide.

Is the t-test valid here?

16:17

A t-test treats each group as a random sample from one population.

Are 30 consecutive Julys like that?

  • [16:17–16:20] The homework extension. Take two or three answers, then add whichever of these the room missed:
  • Normality is not the problem: Shapiro-Wilk p = 0.49 (1881–1910) and 0.077 (1991–2020), and with 30 a side the t-test copes anyway.
  • Independence: consecutive years, but lag-1 autocorrelation is weak (0.15 and −0.17). A hot July doesn’t make next July hot.
  • One population? If the climate drifts within a window, its 30 Julys are not draws from one fixed distribution. 1881–1910 cools by about 0.06 °C a year (p = 0.09). The better question is the trend across all 140 years: regression, Week 7.
  • Same measurement? Same site, instrument and surroundings for 140 years? Anything that changed the readings would look like climate change. Look at the notes sheet; ask the Met Office.
  • Why July? It’s one of twelve months. Ten of the twelve differ at p < 0.02; June and July don’t. Choosing the month after looking is a forking path; testing all twelve is multiple comparisons: Week 8.

Handling NAs

16:25

  • [16:25–16:30] Type it, don’t paste it. Anticipated code:

    sum(c(2, 2, NA))
sum(c(2, 2, NA), na.rm = TRUE)

temp <- c(36, 38, NA, NA, 39, 37, NA, 35)
mean(temp)
mean(temp, na.rm = TRUE)

sum(is.na(temp))

temp_clip <- temp
temp_clip[is.na(temp)] <- Inf
temp_clip
median(temp_clip, na.rm = TRUE)

  • NA means “don’t know”: 1 + 2 + don’t-know is don’t-know (NA). R won’t guess. na.rm = TRUE gives 3.

  • Logger: na.rm = TRUE gives 37 °C. But the three gaps are all above 40 °C, so the true mean is at least 38.1 °C. Dropping them biases the mean low, because they are missing because of their value. Had the battery died at random, dropping them would only cost data.

  • Why not na.omit() everything? It drops a whole row for an NA in any column, even one you aren’t testing, and it hides the question of why. t.test() drops NAs silently: check how many values it used.

  • What to do: count them, find out why (the data’s notes, the supplier), then drop or keep, and say which, and how many, in the write-up.

  • Hands-on version: “Mind the gaps” on the exercise page. Then back to Today’s objectives for the group work.

Before you drop an NA, ask why it’s missing.
